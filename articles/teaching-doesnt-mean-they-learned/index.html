Learning Design

Teaching Doesn’t Mean They Learned

Why information delivery, familiarity, and successful learning are different—and how retrieval, feedback, spacing, and transfer change what we design.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202613 min readShare

Key takeawayExposure is not evidence of learning. A learner can watch, read, nod, highlight, and complete a module while still being unable to retrieve the idea later or use it in a real situation. Learning design begins when we separate delivery from durable performance.

The illusion of successful instruction

Instruction often produces immediate signals that feel reassuring: the slide deck was clear, the learner finished the module, quiz scores were high, the facilitator covered everything, and participants said the session was useful. None of those signals is worthless. None of them, by itself, demonstrates durable learning.

The distinction matters because familiarity is easy to mistake for mastery. When information is still visible, learners can recognize it. When the instructor has just explained the example, a similar question feels easy. When the quiz uses the same wording as the content, learners can often select the right option without reconstructing the underlying idea. The learning problem appears only later—when the cue is gone, the context changes, or the learner must decide what to do.

Roediger and Karpicke’s classic work on test-enhanced learning showed the difference clearly: repeated studying can produce stronger short-term performance and confidence, while retrieval practice produces stronger delayed retention. Dunlosky and colleagues’ broad review similarly identified practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility learning techniques across many learners and materials.

Portfolio lensA strong learning artifact should make a reviewer ask, “What will the learner have to retrieve, decide, or perform without the course in front of them?” If the artifact cannot answer that question, it may be content design rather than learning design.

The 10-second learning test

Remove the explanation. Remove the slide. Remove the highlighted answer. Then ask the learner to produce, choose, explain, diagnose, sequence, or perform something from memory. If the learner cannot do it without the information still on screen, the instruction may have created recognition but not usable knowledge.

This is not a literal ten-second rule for every complex skill. It is a design heuristic: close the book and ask for evidence. The evidence should resemble the future demand.

If the real job requires…Weak evidenceStronger evidence
Recall a safety limitRe-read the limitState or select it later without the reference
Choose a response to a customerWatch a model conversationChoose among plausible responses and explain the consequence
Use softwareWatch a screen recordingComplete the task in the system or a realistic simulation
Diagnose a problemMemorize symptomsInterpret a new case with incomplete information
Teach a conceptRecognize the definitionExplain it in original language and respond to a misconception

Four design moves that turn exposure into learning

1. Retrieval

Ask learners to reconstruct information from memory. Retrieval is not simply an assessment technique; it is itself a learning event. The act of trying to recall strengthens access to the knowledge and reveals what is not yet retrievable.

2. Feedback

Feedback should do more than identify correctness. It should help learners update the mental model behind the answer. In complex decisions, the most useful feedback explains why an option works, what consequence follows, what cue mattered, and what would make a different choice appropriate.

3. Spacing

If a capability matters after Friday, practice should not exist only on Tuesday. Distributed opportunities to retrieve and use knowledge reduce dependence on short-term familiarity. Spacing can be built into a course, a manager conversation, a follow-up scenario, a performance-support prompt, or a later knowledge check.

4. Transfer

Learning succeeds when people can use knowledge under the conditions that matter. That means practice should vary enough to prevent memorizing one example. If every scenario contains the same obvious cue, learners can succeed without learning the underlying discrimination.

Desirable difficulty is not bad UX

Learning should sometimes feel effortful. That does not justify confusing instructions, inaccessible interfaces, trick questions, or overloaded screens. The useful difficulty comes from the thinking: recalling after a delay, distinguishing similar cases, generating an explanation, making a choice with consequences, or applying a rule in a new context.

Bad friction makes the interface harder. Productive difficulty makes the cognition stronger.

Design checkIf the learner is struggling, ask: “Are they struggling with the target skill, or with my interface?” Preserve the first when appropriate. Remove the second.

Objectives should describe evidence, not topics

“Understand the escalation process” is not observable. “Identify when an issue meets escalation criteria and select the correct next action from a new case” tells the designer what evidence to create. The point is not to worship behavioral verbs. The point is to make the expected performance concrete enough that content and assessment can be aligned.

Completion data is operational data, not learning evidence

Completion is useful for compliance tracking, participation, and system administration. It tells us that a learner reached the end. It does not tell us what changed.

A mature measurement strategy separates at least four questions:

  1. Did people participate? Attendance, completion, time, attempts.
  2. Can they demonstrate the capability? Retrieval, decisions, performance, products.
  3. Can they still demonstrate it later? Delayed assessment, follow-up practice.
  4. Did workplace behavior or results change? Quality, speed, errors, adoption, customer outcomes, manager observation.

Worked example: from information deck to learning experience

Imagine a 25-slide orientation deck explaining how supervisors should respond to employee performance concerns. The deck contains definitions, policy excerpts, and a seven-step process. The original training ends with a ten-question multiple-choice quiz using phrases copied from the slides.

A learning redesign might keep some of the information but change the evidence architecture:

Reduce front-loaded explanation.Give learners only the minimum model and a concise job aid.
Add early retrieval.Present a short case and ask which facts matter before revealing the framework.
Use contrasting cases.Include one situation that requires informal coaching and another that requires formal escalation.
Explain consequences.Feedback shows what each decision enables or risks.
Revisit later.A follow-up scenario one week later uses different details but the same governing principles.
Measure workplace use.Managers review whether documentation quality and escalation accuracy improve.

The redesign may contain fewer words than the original deck. It is more demanding because the learner has to think.

Frequently asked questions

Does every lesson need a quiz?

No. Retrieval can be verbal, written, conversational, scenario-based, performance-based, or embedded in a real task. The principle is evidence without the answer visible.

Is recognition ever useful?

Yes. Some tasks genuinely require recognition, and recognition can support early learning. The problem is using recognition as evidence for a performance that later requires recall, judgment, or execution.

How much practice is enough?

Enough to reach the desired level of accuracy and fluency across varied examples, followed by opportunities to retrieve again after time has passed. The exact amount depends on task complexity and consequence.
