Performance + Curriculum

From Problem to Performance

A diagnostic approach to deciding whether training is the solution, what performance evidence matters, and how to design only after the real gap is understood.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202615 min readShare
Audio edition coming soonThis article is ready for its narrated WDW audio edition. The player will activate here when the narration is published.
Key takeawayThe fastest way to waste a learning budget is to begin with “What course should we build?” before establishing whether the problem is caused by knowledge or skill. Performance analysis changes the opening question to “What should people be doing, what are they doing now, and what prevents the desired performance?”

Training requests arrive as solutions

“We need onboarding.” “Build a compliance refresher.” “Managers need communication training.” “People are not using the system correctly.” These statements sound like requirements, but most are solution requests wrapped around an undefined problem.

A learning designer adds value by slowing the solution down long enough to understand the performance. That does not mean creating months of analysis. It means collecting enough evidence to avoid solving the wrong problem efficiently.

The International Society for Performance Improvement has long framed performance improvement as a systematic discipline that looks beyond instruction to the conditions in which people perform. That perspective is especially important in corporate learning, where incentives, tools, processes, role clarity, workload, permissions, and manager behavior can overwhelm even excellent training.

Portfolio lensA senior-level case study should show the reasoning before the artifact: the original request, evidence collected, performance gap, non-training causes, design decision, intervention mix, and measurement plan. The finished course is only one part of the story.

A simple diagnostic equation

Performance gapDesired performance − Current performance = Gap

The next question is not “How large is the gap?” It is “Why does the gap exist?”

That second question prevents the training department from becoming a content factory. If people already know how to perform but the workflow requires six systems, conflicting approvals, and a metric that rewards speed over quality, another module is unlikely to fix the problem.

Six causes to investigate before prescribing instruction

CauseDiagnostic questionTypical response
KnowledgeDo people know what to do and why?Instruction, explanation, reference
Skill / fluencyCan they perform reliably under realistic conditions?Practice, coaching, simulation, feedback
Tools / environmentDoes the system make the right behavior difficult?UX, job aids, system redesign, access
Process / role clarityAre ownership, sequence, and decision rights clear?Process redesign, SOP, role clarification
Motivation / consequenceWhat happens when people do or do not perform?Feedback, incentives, accountability, manager action
CapacityDo people have time, staffing, and cognitive bandwidth?Workload, staffing, prioritization, simplification

The stakeholder questions that change the design

Good discovery questions are concrete. “What do learners need to know?” invites a content list. “What does a strong performer do differently in the moment?” invites performance evidence.

  • What observable behavior would tell you the problem is solved?
  • What is a strong performer doing today that a struggling performer is not?
  • What mistakes are most costly?
  • What information is available at the moment of performance?
  • What decisions cannot be looked up and therefore require internalized knowledge?
  • What happens immediately after the desired behavior?
  • Which constraints are outside the employee’s control?
  • What data already exists—error logs, support tickets, quality reviews, customer complaints, rework, cycle time?

Minimum viable analysis

Not every project needs a formal research study. A practical minimum viable analysis can often be completed with four evidence streams:

Stakeholder interview.Clarify business result, target behavior, constraints, and consequence.
Performer comparison.Observe or interview strong and struggling performers to identify behavior differences.
Artifact review.Examine SOPs, tickets, dashboards, forms, systems, policies, and existing training.
Data check.Find at least one baseline measure connected to the problem.

This is usually enough to produce a more defensible first hypothesis than beginning with a slide deck.

Write performance outcomes before learning objectives

Learning objectives describe what the learner will demonstrate during or after learning. Performance outcomes connect that demonstration to the workplace. For example:

Weak request: Understand the customer escalation policy.

Learning evidence: Given a new customer case, identify whether escalation criteria are met and select the correct next action.

Performance outcome: Reduce incorrect escalations and missed required escalations while maintaining response time.

The last line changes the project. It tells us what workplace data should eventually move.

Measurement should begin before design

If measurement is discussed only after launch, teams often settle for whatever the LMS already captures. Starting with the performance outcome makes measurement part of design.

Measurement chainBusiness signal → target behavior → practice evidence → workplace evidence → review cadence.

For a software-adoption project, the business signal might be support volume, the behavior might be successful completion of a key workflow, the practice evidence might be task completion in simulation, and the workplace evidence might be product telemetry or error rates.

Worked example: “Our managers need communication training”

Suppose employee survey comments say managers do not communicate priorities clearly. The default response is a communication course. A diagnostic approach asks what “clear” means behaviorally. Interviews reveal that managers can explain priorities, but priorities change weekly, two senior leaders send conflicting instructions, and the project-management system is not kept current. Employees therefore rely on informal messages.

The intervention changes. Managers may still need a short skill component, but the larger solution is a weekly prioritization cadence, a single source of truth, decision ownership, and a manager conversation guide. Training becomes one element of a performance system rather than the entire answer.

Frequently asked questions

Does analysis slow projects down?

Bad analysis can. Focused analysis often saves time because it prevents unnecessary production, reduces revision, and makes stakeholder decisions explicit earlier.

What if the client insists on a course?

Meet the request while documenting the diagnosis. You can often improve the course by adding performance support, realistic practice, manager reinforcement, or measurement even when the broader system cannot be redesigned immediately.

When is training clearly appropriate?

When desired performance depends on knowledge, skill, judgment, or fluency that people do not yet possess—and the environment allows them to use it.
