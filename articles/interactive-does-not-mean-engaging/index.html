Interactive Does Not Automatically Mean Engaging

Why clicks, tabs, motion, drag-and-drop, and gamification only matter when they make the learner retrieve, decide, compare, explain, or practice.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…
The mind is the interfaceClicking ≠ thinking
Key takeawayVisible activity is not the same as cognitive engagement. The best eLearning interactions require the learner to do the kind of thinking the real performance requires.

Clicking is behavior. Engagement is cognitive.

There is a moment in almost every eLearning project when someone asks, “Can we make this more interactive?” The request usually comes from a good place. Nobody wants a learner staring at endless text and clicking Next for forty minutes. But the industry has spent years confusing interaction with engagement.

A tab is interactive. A flip card is interactive. Drag-and-drop is interactive. A spinning icon is interactive. None of those things automatically require meaningful thought.

My teaching background made this distinction hard to ignore. A classroom can be noisy, active, and full of participation while very little learning is happening. Another room can be quiet while students wrestle deeply with an idea. The visible behavior is not the same as the mental work.

Digital learning has the same problem. The strongest question is not “What can the learner click?” It is “What should the learner notice, retrieve, decide, compare, explain, or practice?”

Meaningful interaction changes the learner’s mental state

An instructional interaction earns its place when it helps produce a useful cognitive action. Retrieval asks the learner to bring knowledge back without seeing it. Comparison asks them to notice differences. Classification asks them to apply criteria. Prediction asks them to build a model of what will happen. Decision-making asks them to weigh cues and consequences.

Those interactions can be visually simple. A well-designed question with two plausible options can create more learning than an expensive animation if the choice reveals judgment.

Interaction testRemove the animation and ask what thinking remains. If almost nothing remains, the interaction was probably decorative.

The button problem

Many authoring tools make it easy to build buttons, states, triggers, layers, motion, and branching. That is useful. It also means the tool can drive the pedagogy. Designers start asking what the software can do instead of what the learner needs to do.

A button is not an instructional strategy. It is a control. The learning value comes from what the control enables. Reveal an example? Compare two cases? Commit to a choice? Hear a customer response? Inspect evidence? Practice a sequence? The verb after the click matters more than the click.

Attention is not the same as entertainment

We do need attention. A learner who leaves the course cannot learn from it. But attention can be earned through relevance, uncertainty, challenge, story, useful surprise, clear design, and the feeling of making progress. We do not have to turn every lesson into a game show.

Entertainment can support learning, but novelty wears off. If every screen moves, motion becomes background noise. If every paragraph has a reveal, the learner may focus more on operating the interface than understanding the content.

Scenarios are powerful because they make knowledge answer to a situation

Scenario-based learning is one of the most valuable forms of eLearning interaction because it can place knowledge inside a decision. Instead of asking, “What does the policy say?” we can ask, “What would you do now?”

The scenario becomes stronger when options are plausible and consequences teach something. The learner should need to interpret cues, not identify the obviously ridiculous answer. Feedback should explain the consequence, reveal the principle, or let the learner recover.

This is why branching scenarios are central to the wdw.llc portfolio approach. They show instructional design as decision architecture, not merely screen design.

Retrieval is interaction even when the screen barely moves

Some of the most effective learning actions are visually plain. Ask the learner to recall three warning signs before revealing them. Ask them to explain a concept in one sentence. Ask them to predict which customer case will escalate. Ask them to compare their answer with an expert model.

These actions make the learner do cognitive work before the system supplies the answer. That is interaction in the deepest sense.

Feedback is where interaction becomes instruction

A choice without feedback can become guessing. Feedback without another attempt can become commentary. Strong interactive learning closes the loop.

When a learner chooses, the system should respond in a way that helps them refine the mental model. Sometimes that means explaining why. Sometimes it means showing a realistic consequence. Sometimes it means asking the learner to try again with a new clue.

“Incorrect. Try again.” is technically responsive and instructionally thin.

Agency is more than choosing a path

Designers often describe branching as agency because the learner selects among options. Real agency requires that choices matter. If every route returns immediately to the same screen and the choice has no consequence, the learner learns that choice is cosmetic.

Meaningful agency can be small. Let the learner choose which case to investigate, decide what question to ask first, select a tool, allocate limited time, or decide when to escalate. The experience becomes a model of work rather than a slideshow with detours.

Gamification can support progress, but points are not purpose

Badges, points, levels, timers, and leaderboards can create energy. They can also redirect attention toward the reward rather than the capability. The question is what behavior the game mechanic reinforces.

A progress indicator that helps a learner see movement can be useful. A timer can create realistic pressure if the job requires speed. A score can help when it represents meaningful quality. A leaderboard may be harmful if the task depends on collaboration or if public comparison discourages people who need practice most.

Game mechanics should serve the learning model, not replace it.

Multimedia needs a job too

Video, animation, audio, diagrams, and sound can clarify processes, model performance, create context, or make abstract relationships visible. They can also overwhelm working memory when they compete for attention.

A narrated animation may help explain a dynamic system. Background music may create tone without interfering with narration. A decorative talking avatar may simply add another channel the learner must process.

Production decisions should begin with instructional purpose. Beautiful is welcome. Beautiful and purposeful is better.

On mobile, every interaction becomes a usability test

Hover does not exist in the same way. Drag-and-drop can become frustrating. Tiny hotspots punish large fingers. Fixed panels consume precious space. Landscape interactions can collapse on a portrait screen.

If mobile matters, test real devices early. Ask whether the interaction can be completed with keyboard, touch, zoom, and assistive technology. An interaction that works only in the designer’s browser is not finished.

Accessibility reveals whether interaction was designed or decorated

Keyboard access, visible focus, labels, target size, captions, alternatives to drag interactions, and motion controls are not secondary details. They expose the structure of the interaction.

WCAG 2.2 helps teams identify many of these requirements. UDL adds a broader learning-design question: can learners perceive, engage with, and act on the experience in meaningful ways?

Use interaction data carefully

Digital learning can record clicks, paths, attempts, time, and choices. More data does not automatically mean more understanding. Time on page may indicate engagement, confusion, interruption, or a coffee break.

The most useful data is connected to a hypothesis. If learners repeatedly choose the same wrong branch, that may reveal a misconception. If they abandon a drag interaction on mobile, that may reveal usability. If they replay a video section, it may identify complexity worth examining.

A five-minute interaction audit

  1. List every interaction in the experience.
  2. Write the cognitive verb it requires: retrieve, compare, decide, explain, apply, inspect, create.
  3. Circle interactions whose verb is only click, reveal, drag, or advance.
  4. Ask whether each interaction changes understanding or provides useful evidence.
  5. Remove, simplify, or redesign anything that consumes attention without doing instructional work.

A before-and-after example

Before: Learners click five tabs to reveal the five steps of a difficult customer conversation. Then they answer a multiple-choice question asking which step comes third.

After: Learners hear the opening of a realistic call, choose what to say next, see the customer response, and then compare the choice with a five-step conversation framework. Later they face a different customer who requires the same underlying strategy.

Both versions are interactive. Only one resembles the performance.

The goal is not a busy screen. It is a busy mind.

Good eLearning can be elegant, animated, beautiful, and entertaining. I want all of those things when they help. But the center of the experience is still the learner thinking.

If the design makes the learner retrieve, discriminate, decide, explain, practice, and transfer, the interaction has a job. If it only makes the learner click, we may have built activity without learning.

There are several kinds of engagement, and they should not be confused

Behavioral engagement is visible participation. Emotional engagement is interest, curiosity, belonging, or investment. Cognitive engagement is the mental effort directed toward understanding and solving. A learner can display one without the others.

A person may click every button and think very little. Another may pause on one diagram for five minutes and build a deep mental model. Analytics will record the first learner as “active” unless the design is smarter about what activity means.

Some friction is instructional

Designers are trained to remove friction from user experience. Learning introduces a complication: some difficulty is desirable because retrieval, generation, comparison, and problem solving require effort.

The task is to remove interface friction while preserving cognitive challenge. The learner should not struggle to find the Next button. They may need to struggle to decide which principle applies.

Micro-interactions should orient, confirm, or teach

Hover states, button motion, progress indicators, transitions, and small animations can make an interface feel responsive. They should communicate something: this is clickable, your action succeeded, you moved to a new stage, this item is active.

Motion that has no communicative role should be restrained around dense learning content. The interface can feel alive without constantly competing with the idea.

More interaction can create choice overload

Giving learners multiple panels, menus, filters, paths, and media controls can create a feeling of freedom while increasing navigation burden. Choices should be meaningful enough to justify attention.

A good rule is progressive disclosure: show what is needed now, reveal complexity when the learner is ready, and keep the mental model of the interface stable.

Story engages because it creates unanswered questions

Stories generate attention through causality and uncertainty. What happens next? Why did that person act that way? What would I do? That makes narrative especially useful in scenario-based learning.

The story should not merely decorate information. It should create a problem the learner needs the content to solve.

Social interaction can be more valuable than screen interaction

Sometimes the best “interactive” component is a conversation between people. Ask learners to compare decisions, critique examples, teach a concept to a partner, or negotiate a shared response. The digital experience can prompt and structure the social learning without pretending the screen must do everything.

Measure engagement through evidence of thinking

Instead of counting clicks alone, look at explanations, decisions, revisions, retrieval attempts, time on meaningful tasks, confidence changes, and transfer performance. Those indicators are closer to the learning process.

Even then, interpret carefully. A long time may mean deep thinking or confusion. Data needs context.

Ask one question for every interaction

What would the learner lose if this interaction became plain text? If the answer is nothing, simplify. If the interaction creates retrieval, comparison, consequence, agency, or useful feedback, keep it and make it excellent.

Interactivity is expensive in attention even when it is cheap in software. Spend attention like a limited resource.

Engagement is not the number of things a learner touches

A course can contain buttons, tabs, sliders, hotspots, animations, drag interactions, and still ask the learner to do almost no meaningful thinking. That is the trap. We can make the screen busy while the mind remains passive.

When I evaluate an interaction, I ask what changes cognitively because the learner interacted. Did the learner retrieve something? Compare two possibilities? Predict a consequence? Notice a pattern? Explain a choice? If the answer is no, the interaction may be interface activity rather than learning activity.

This distinction matters because development time is limited. Every custom interaction competes with the time we could spend improving the scenario, feedback, examples, or assessment.

A meaningful choice needs plausible alternatives

Weak scenarios often advertise the correct answer. One option is obviously respectful and professional. Another is absurdly rude. The learner is technically making a choice, but the decision has no diagnostic value.

Strong alternatives represent reasonable misunderstandings, competing priorities, partial truths, or shortcuts a real person might take. The learner has to interpret the cues rather than spot the “good” sentence.

That is also what makes feedback worth reading. If the distractor is plausible, feedback can explain why it fails under these conditions and when a similar action might be appropriate elsewhere.

Narrative is useful when it creates stakes

Stories are not automatically engaging either. A scenario becomes valuable when the narrative gives context to the decision. Who is involved? What do they care about? What has already happened? What information is incomplete? What will change because of this choice?

Those details create a reason to think. They make abstract principles visible in human situations.

The best story elements are therefore functional. They establish motive, constraint, emotion, or consequence. Decorative backstory that never affects a decision can usually be shortened.

Use voice, music, and motion to carry information or emotion with purpose

Audio can make a difficult conversation feel different from reading a transcript. A pause, a strained tone, or hesitation may become a cue the learner must interpret. Music can establish mood in reflective content. Animation can reveal a process over time.

The question is whether the medium is doing instructional work. If the same decision would mean exactly the same thing without the media, the extra production may still be aesthetically worthwhile, but it should not be confused with deeper learning.

This is where tools like ElevenLabs can be useful. Expressive voice and sound can expand what a small production team can create. The design still has to decide what the learner should notice and do.

Measure engagement through behavior that matters

Time on page, clicks, and completion can tell us something about use. They do not tell us whether the learner was cognitively engaged. A learner can click every object and remember nothing. Another can spend less time because they understood the model quickly.

Look for stronger evidence: quality of decisions, explanation, return visits to useful support, improvement across attempts, delayed recall, transfer to a different case, or real-world behavior after the experience.

Give every interaction a budget

One practical discipline is to treat custom interactions as if they cost real money even when the software makes them easy. What learning value justifies the build? What accessibility work does it add? What testing does it require? What happens on mobile? What maintenance burden does it create?

If the answer is “it makes the page less boring,” I would rather improve the example, sharpen the writing, or ask a better question.

Interactivity becomes powerful when it makes thinking visible.

Classify the interaction by what the mind must do

A click is a motor action. Learning depends on the mental action around the click. That distinction gives designers a better way to evaluate interaction.

Wayne’s field noteIn classrooms, activity can fool us. A room can be noisy, colorful, and full of movement while very little difficult thinking is happening. Digital learning has the same trap. The designer has to ask what the learner is mentally doing, not how many things are moving on the screen.

Good interaction creates productive friction

The best interaction often slows the learner down for a useful reason. A scenario makes them commit to a choice. A comparison asks them to notice a pattern. A prediction makes them expose a mental model before the explanation appears. That small amount of friction creates a reason to think.

Unproductive friction is different. Hidden buttons, confusing drag targets, unnecessary animation, and novelty interactions consume attention without improving understanding. Remove those aggressively.

Connect choice to consequence

If a learner makes a decision, let the experience show why the decision matters. Feedback can reveal a customer reaction, a process breakdown, a safety implication, a team consequence, or a change in available options. Consequence turns clicking into simulation.

This is one reason branching scenarios are powerful when used well. The branch is not valuable because the screen changed. It is valuable because the learner can test a judgment and see a believable result.

Use the minimum interaction needed for the learning job

Some content should simply be read. Some ideas are best explained with a diagram. Some decisions deserve a simulation. Interaction is not a virtue by itself. It is a design resource with costs in development time, accessibility, testing, and cognitive load.

Choose it when the learner needs to retrieve, discriminate, decide, practice, explain, or create. Otherwise, clarity may be the more engaging design.

Run the engagement check after the novelty wears off

When reviewing an interaction, imagine that the visual novelty has disappeared. Remove the animation, sound effect, hover state, and decorative motion in your mind. What remains for the learner to think about? If the answer is still a meaningful decision, comparison, retrieval, explanation, or act of creation, the interaction has instructional substance. If the answer is merely “click to continue,” the engagement is mostly cosmetic.

This check is especially useful during stakeholder review because visually impressive interactions can attract disproportionate attention. A designer should be able to explain the mental action the interaction is intended to produce and what evidence will show whether that action happened.

Engagement is not the number of times the learner touches the screen. It is sustained, purposeful attention directed toward the thing worth learning.
