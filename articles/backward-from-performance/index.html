Performance + Curriculum

Backward from Performance: Curriculum That Starts with What People Must Do

A performance-first approach to curriculum design that begins with real capability, evidence, and transfer instead of a pile of content waiting to be covered.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…

Key takeawayCurriculum is stronger when it is built backward from observable performance and credible evidence. Content should enter because the capability requires it, not because the source material happens to exist.

Curriculum should begin at the far end of the learning experience

When I design curriculum, I try to resist the gravitational pull of content. Content is seductive because it is visible. We have chapters, standards, subject-matter experts, decks, policies, videos, and documents waiting to be organized. The easiest thing to do is start sequencing them.

The harder and better question is: What should a capable person be able to do when all of this is over? That question moves curriculum from coverage to performance.

Understanding by Design popularized backward design in education: identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, then plan learning experiences. The same logic is powerful in professional learning. Begin with the performance, decide what evidence would prove it, then design instruction that makes that evidence more likely.

Coverage creates the illusion of completeness

A curriculum can cover every required topic and still leave people unable to perform. Coverage answers, “Was it included?” Performance asks, “Can the learner use it?” Those are different standards.

I have seen students sit through explanations, take notes, complete worksheets, and then struggle when asked to apply the idea in an unfamiliar situation. The missing ingredient was not more coverage. It was practice in the kind of thinking the final performance required.

Backward performance designPerformance → Evidence → Practice → Knowledge → Content.

Content enters because the performance requires it, not because it exists.

Write the performance before the objective

Objectives often become abstract: understand, know, appreciate, become familiar with. Before refining the objective, describe the moment of use. What is the person doing? What information is available? What constraints exist? What decision must be made? What does good performance look like?

“Understand escalation policy” becomes “Given a customer case, identify whether escalation criteria are met, choose the correct route, and document the rationale.” Now the curriculum has a destination.

Evidence should look like the real capability

If the final job requires judgment, the assessment should include judgment. If the job requires troubleshooting, the assessment should include imperfect situations and competing clues. If the job requires explanation, the learner should explain. A recall quiz can support learning, but it cannot stand in for every form of evidence.

This is where curriculum alignment becomes concrete. We compare what we say matters with what we actually ask learners to do.

Separate what must be remembered from what can be found

Performance-based curriculum benefits from a simple knowledge inventory. What must be available from memory because the person needs it quickly or uses it constantly? What can be supported by a reference because accuracy matters more than recall? What can be automated safely? What requires contextual judgment?

This distinction prevents the course from becoming a storage contest. Learners need mental models and critical knowledge. They also need well-designed tools.

Sequence by dependency, not by the table of contents

Good sequence answers, “What does the learner need before this can make sense?” Sometimes the sequence is conceptual. Sometimes it follows the workflow. Sometimes it moves from common cases to exceptions, whole task to component practice, or concrete examples to abstraction.

The source manual’s chapter order is rarely a sufficient instructional sequence. Documentation is organized for reference. Learning needs to be organized for development.

Scaffold the performance, then remove the scaffold

At the beginning, learners may need worked examples, cues, checklists, simplified cases, or step-by-step guidance. If those supports never fade, we have taught performance with support, not independent performance.

Backward design makes fading easier because independence is visible from the start. We know what the learner will eventually have to do without the training environment.

Design backward from transfer, not completion

Transfer means the capability survives after the course and under changed conditions. That requires variation. If every practice item looks like the example, learners may memorize the surface pattern. Real work rarely announces which lesson applies.

Build cases that change irrelevant features while preserving the underlying decision. Ask learners to explain why. Include borderline cases. Let them compare two plausible options. Transfer grows when learners practice recognizing structure, not merely repeating a procedure.

Curriculum is a system of repeated opportunities

A single lesson can introduce a capability. Curriculum is where capabilities are revisited, deepened, combined, and transferred. That is why mapping matters. We need to know where an idea first appears, where it is practiced, where support fades, and where learners must integrate it with other skills.

Without that map, repetition can be accidental and gaps can hide between courses.

AI changes content production, not the need for backward design

Generative AI makes it possible to create more content faster than ever. That makes backward design more important because content scarcity is no longer the constraint. Judgment about what deserves to exist becomes the constraint.

A model can generate fifty examples. The designer still has to decide which examples create useful variation, which contain errors, which match the learner’s context, and which assessment actually proves competence.

When production becomes cheap, alignment becomes premium.

Backward design improves stakeholder conversations

Stakeholders often arrive with content requests because content is what they can hand us. “Put this policy into training.” “Turn this deck into eLearning.” “Make a course on communication.” A performance conversation changes the request.

Ask what people should do differently after the experience. Ask what evidence would convince the stakeholder that the initiative worked. Ask what currently prevents that performance. Those questions move the conversation from deliverable to outcome.

The teacher’s version of backward design

Long before I used formal instructional-design language, teaching pushed me toward backward thinking. If I wanted students to analyze evidence, I could not spend the entire unit asking them to copy notes. If I wanted them to defend an interpretation, they needed repeated practice making claims, selecting evidence, hearing counterarguments, and revising.

The final task exposes the truth about the curriculum. If the assessment asks for thinking the instruction never rehearsed, the learner is not the only one being tested. The design is being tested too.

A practical backward curriculum protocol

  1. Describe the real performance in context.
  2. Define acceptable evidence and quality criteria.
  3. Identify prerequisite knowledge, skill, and judgment.
  4. Separate memory requirements from performance support.
  5. Design practice that increasingly resembles the performance.
  6. Add variation for transfer.
  7. Fade scaffolds.
  8. Map where the capability is introduced, practiced, integrated, and demonstrated.
  9. Measure whether performance changes after instruction.

A curriculum is not a container for information

It is a designed pathway toward capability. When we begin with the end, content becomes purposeful, assessment becomes evidence, practice becomes more authentic, and learners have a better chance of leaving with something they can actually use.

The curriculum does not need to contain everything. It needs to develop what matters.

Define quality before you design practice

A performance statement tells us what someone should do. A quality criterion tells us what good looks like. Without criteria, learners can complete the task without knowing what distinguishes competent from excellent work.

Criteria should be observable enough to guide feedback. Instead of “communicates effectively,” specify behaviors: confirms the issue, explains the next step, checks understanding, documents the decision accurately.

Exemplars make abstract quality visible

Show a strong example, a borderline one, and a flawed one. Ask learners to compare them against the criteria. This is often more powerful than giving a rubric alone because the learner sees how quality operates in context.

Exemplars also help subject matter experts reveal tacit standards they may not have articulated.

Map prerequisites backward too

If the final performance requires five forms of knowledge, three judgments, and one tool skill, do not teach them in isolation without showing how they combine. Map dependencies: what must be understood before the next element can make sense?

This creates a curriculum spine. Supporting content can be added around the spine without losing direction.

Use whole, part, whole for complex performance

Show the complete performance early so learners understand the destination. Then isolate difficult components for focused practice. Return to the whole task and integrate them.

This pattern protects against a common curriculum problem where learners master pieces but never learn to coordinate them.

Build choice around route, not destination

Learners may need different amounts of practice. Diagnostic checks can let experienced learners move faster while novices receive more support. The final performance standard can remain common even when the route differs.

This is where UDL and backward design work well together: stable goals, flexible pathways.

Treat curriculum maintenance as part of the design

Policies change, tools change, evidence changes, and organizations learn. A curriculum should have review dates, owners, source records, and a process for deciding whether a change affects content, practice, assessment, or all three.

Evergreen curriculum is not curriculum that never changes. It is curriculum built so change can be managed without losing alignment.

The end keeps the middle honest

Whenever a project starts drifting toward “include this too,” return to the performance and evidence. Does the new content help the learner do the thing? Does it support a prerequisite? Does it reduce a known barrier?

Backward design is powerful partly because it gives the team a principled way to say no.

Start with the moment that matters

When I think about a learning experience, I try to picture the learner after the course has disappeared. There is no slide deck open. No facilitator is standing beside them. The learner is in the actual environment, under the actual time pressure, with the actual consequences of getting the decision right or wrong.

That moment gives us better design information than a list of topics. If a supervisor must recognize when coaching has become a documentation issue, the design target is not “understand performance management.” It is the ability to notice cues, distinguish similar cases, choose a next action, and explain the reasoning. If a customer-service representative must de-escalate a tense call, the target is not “know the communication model.” It is selecting and delivering an appropriate response while interpreting tone and context.

Backward design from performance makes the course smaller and the learning stronger because every content decision has to answer a simple question: What does this help the learner do?

Define evidence before activities

Teams often jump from objective to activity: “We need a video, a scenario, a quiz, and a job aid.” I prefer to put another step in between. What evidence would convince us the learner can perform?

Evidence may be a completed procedure, a defensible choice, an explanation, a product, a conversation, a diagnosis, or a sequence of actions. Once that evidence is clear, the activity becomes easier to select. A scenario is appropriate when judgment matters. A worked example is useful when the learner needs a model. A checklist is useful when memory should not be the bottleneck. A simulation is valuable when execution under realistic conditions matters.

Include the conditions and constraints of real performance

Performance is not only the action. It includes the environment in which the action must occur. Can the learner use a reference? Is time limited? Are multiple systems open? Is the information incomplete? Does another person react? Are there legal, ethical, or safety constraints?

If the real task allows a job aid, forcing memorization in the course may create artificial difficulty. If the real task requires immediate recall, an open-book quiz may underestimate the demand. If the real task requires judgment under ambiguity, a quiz with one obviously correct answer will not prepare the learner for the gray area.

Design becomes more authentic when practice inherits the meaningful constraints of the job and removes the irrelevant ones.

Teach the minimum model that supports good decisions

Subject matter experts know a great deal, and expertise makes it difficult to see which parts are necessary for a beginner. That is why topic-first courses tend to grow. Everything feels important because everything is connected to the domain.

Performance-first design gives us a filter. Learners need the concepts, cues, procedures, examples, and exceptions that support the target performance. The rest may belong in a reference library, advanced course, searchable knowledge base, or later learning path.

This is not anti-content. It is pro-purpose. A smaller set of well-chosen ideas that learners can retrieve and use is more valuable than a comprehensive tour they cannot apply.

Performance-first thinking is visible in a strong portfolio

A portfolio should show more than attractive screens. It should make the reasoning visible: what problem existed, what people needed to do, what evidence counted, what constraints shaped the design, and why the chosen experience was appropriate.

That is one reason I like branching scenarios as portfolio artifacts. They reveal the underlying performance model. The branches show what cues matter, which decisions are plausible, how consequences differ, and how feedback guides the next attempt.

The visual production is important. The design logic is what makes the piece instructional design.

Performance changes, so the learning system needs maintenance

Processes change. Software changes. Policy changes. Customer expectations change. AI changes workflows quickly enough that a course can become outdated while still looking polished.

If the design is anchored to performance, maintenance becomes easier. We can ask whether the required decisions, tools, conditions, and evidence have changed. If they have, we update the practice and supporting information. If they have not, a cosmetic change may not be urgent.

That is another advantage of designing backward from the job: it gives the learning product a stable reason for existing and a clear trigger for revision.

Start with the moment of performance

Backward design becomes much more practical when the starting point is not a standard, topic, or slide deck but a moment in which someone must do something observable. Picture the learner after the training. What are they looking at? What decision do they need to make? What information is available? What makes the situation difficult? What would a strong performer notice that a novice might miss?

That scene gives the curriculum direction. Instead of asking how much content can fit into the experience, ask what knowledge and practice are necessary to succeed in that moment.

Use performance as a content filter

Content expands naturally. Every subject-matter expert remembers one more exception, one more history point, one more policy note, one more screen to explain. Performance gives the designer a disciplined way to decide what belongs.

If a piece of information changes a decision, supports a required explanation, prevents a predictable error, or helps the learner complete the task, it probably belongs. If it is merely interesting, it may belong in a reference layer rather than the core learning path.

Wayne’s field noteTeachers know this tension well. There is always more worth knowing than time available to teach. Good curriculum is not the art of squeezing everything in. It is the art of deciding what learners must leave able to do and then protecting the experiences that make that performance possible.

Run an alignment check before development

Take the objective, the practice, and the assessment and place them side by side. If the objective says “analyze,” but the practice asks learners to watch and the assessment asks them to recall a definition, the curriculum is not aligned. The verbs do not need to match literally, but the cognitive and performance demands should.

This check is especially useful in AI-assisted production. Generative tools can create large amounts of activity quickly. Alignment prevents the project from becoming a collection of plausible but disconnected pieces.

Separate what must be learned from what can be supported

Not everything needs to be memorized. If a performer can safely use a checklist, calculator, reference table, or decision guide during the real task, practice should include those supports rather than pretending they do not exist.

This changes curriculum design in a healthy way. The learning experience can focus memory on concepts, patterns, and decisions while external tools carry details that are better looked up. That is more faithful to modern knowledge work and often reduces unnecessary cognitive load.

Curriculum becomes an architecture of performance

When built backward, a curriculum is no longer a sequence of topics. It is an architecture that prepares people for increasingly authentic performance. Early experiences build concepts. Middle experiences combine them. Later experiences introduce ambiguity, time pressure, exceptions, and realistic decisions. Assessment becomes evidence of capability rather than a separate event at the end.

This is the logic I want visible in any strong portfolio: the content exists because the performance requires it, not because the designer happened to have material available.
