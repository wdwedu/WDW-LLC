Designing for Variability: UDL, Accessibility, and the Myth of the Average Learner

How UDL, accessibility, mobile design, and a clearer definition of rigor can help instructional designers build for real human variability instead of an imaginary average learner.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…
Variability is predictableAccess without lowering the bar
Key takeawayInclusive design is not about making learning easier. It is about preserving the essential challenge while removing barriers that have nothing to do with the capability we actually want learners to develop.

There is no useful “average learner” waiting for your course

One of the most persistent design mistakes is imagining a learner who exists mostly in our heads: enough background knowledge, enough time, enough reading fluency, perfect vision, reliable internet, no distractions, no anxiety about the topic, and exactly the device we tested on. Then a real human being opens the course.

After years in classrooms, curriculum work, special education settings, and online learning, I stopped being surprised by variability. The surprise would be sameness. Two people can sit in the same room, hear the same explanation, complete the same activity, and need completely different routes to strong performance.

Universal Design for Learning begins from a useful premise: variability is predictable. Accessibility standards add another discipline: digital content should not require a particular body, sense, input method, or browser behavior in order to be usable. Together, these ideas push instructional designers away from retrofitting accommodations and toward designing fewer unnecessary barriers in the first place.

Access is not the same thing as lowering rigor

When people hear “multiple means” or “accessibility,” they sometimes picture simplified work. That is a category error. A ramp does not change the destination. Captions do not make the argument easier. Keyboard navigation does not reduce the complexity of the simulation. A graphic organizer can reduce irrelevant memory load while leaving the reasoning demand intact.

The design question is: What is the actual construct we want to measure? If the goal is to analyze historical causation, are we accidentally measuring how quickly someone can decode a dense block of small text? If the goal is to troubleshoot a process, are we accidentally measuring fine-motor precision with a mouse? If the goal is spoken customer interaction, then speech may be central. If it is not, forcing one response mode may add noise.

Preserve the challenge, remove the irrelevant barrier.Rigor belongs in the thinking, decision, performance, or creation the learner must do. Friction that does not serve that goal is not rigor. It is just friction.

UDL 3.0 shifts the conversation toward learner agency

CAST’s UDL Guidelines 3.0 describe the goal as learner agency that is purposeful and reflective, resourceful and authentic, strategic and action-oriented. That language matters. The goal is not to create a course with a checklist of optional buttons. The goal is to help more learners enter, persist, understand, act, and increasingly regulate their own learning.

UDL commonly organizes design attention around engagement, representation, and action and expression. In practice, I treat those as questions. How can learners connect meaningfully to the goal? How can essential information be perceived and understood? How can learners act, communicate, and demonstrate learning without irrelevant barriers?

Options should not become clutter. Giving learners six versions of everything can create decision fatigue. The designer’s job is to provide meaningful flexibility around predictable barriers, not decorate the interface with choice.

Accessibility is a baseline, not a bonus feature

WCAG 2.2 provides testable guidance for making web content more accessible to people with disabilities across devices. For learning teams, that means accessibility cannot live only in the final quality-assurance pass. Color contrast, focus order, captions, labels, semantic structure, target size, error handling, motion, and keyboard operation are design decisions.

If we wait until the end, accessibility becomes expensive because the content architecture is already fixed. If we plan it early, many decisions are ordinary: use real headings, write meaningful link text, do not place essential information in color alone, provide captions and transcripts, preserve visible focus, let people pause motion, and make interactions operable without a mouse.

Accessibility also improves usability for people who may not identify as disabled. Captions help in a noisy room. Transcripts support search and review. Clear headings help everyone scan. Larger targets help someone using a phone while moving. Flexible timing helps a learner dealing with interruptions.

Variability shows up before the learner even reaches the content

Designers often think about prior knowledge but underweight other forms of variability. Learners differ in language background, cultural familiarity, confidence, sensory access, executive-function demands, available technology, motivation, fatigue, and the amount of uninterrupted time they can control.

Those factors interact. A learner using a second language on a small phone during a commute may appear to have a “comprehension problem” when the design problem is density, context, and device constraints. A person with deep expertise may appear disengaged because the course forces them through material they already know. A beginner may appear careless because the task requires distinctions that have never been made visible.

This is why persona work should describe conditions, not stereotypes. “Busy manager” is less useful than “has ten-minute windows, needs to make a decision during the workday, already knows the policy language, but struggles to recognize exceptions.”

Choice is useful when it serves the goal

Choice can increase relevance and autonomy, but not every choice improves learning. Letting learners choose blue or green buttons is personalization without meaning. Letting them choose a practice context relevant to their role may improve transfer. Letting them choose audio, text, or both may support access to the same core idea.

A good option answers a barrier. A bad option adds complexity without changing the learning relationship.

What special education taught me about “fixing the learner”

Special education can sharpen a designer’s eye because barriers become impossible to ignore. But the most useful lesson I carried forward was not a list of accommodations. It was the habit of asking whether the environment was creating part of the difficulty.

Sometimes the learner needs targeted instruction. Sometimes the task needs to change. Sometimes the goal is appropriate but the representation is inaccessible. Sometimes the person needs assistive technology. Sometimes expectations are unclear. The diagnosis matters because different problems require different supports.

That mindset transfers directly into instructional design. We should resist explanations that locate every failure inside the learner. “They were not motivated” may be true. It may also be a convenient way to avoid examining relevance, workload, incentives, usability, belonging, and prior success.

Accessibility and cognitive load often meet in the interface

Dense screens, decorative motion, weak hierarchy, unclear navigation, and overloaded interactions create avoidable processing demands. Those demands affect everyone, but they can disproportionately affect learners with attention, vision, language, or executive-function challenges.

A clean interface is not merely aesthetic. It communicates structure. White space tells the eye what belongs together. Headings create a map. Consistent controls reduce relearning. Progressive disclosure lets the learner focus on what matters now. Good visual design is part of instructional design when it protects attention for the task.

Design for the phone before the learner proves they own a laptop

Many learning experiences are technically responsive but practically miserable on a phone. Text shrinks, tables spill sideways, hover interactions disappear, drag-and-drop becomes precision work, and fixed navigation consumes half the viewport.

Mobile design forces useful discipline. What is essential? Can the task be completed with one hand? Are targets large enough? Does the interaction rely on hover? Can the learner zoom without breaking the layout? Can the content survive a narrower line length? If the answer is no, “responsive” may be only a CSS claim.

Assessment must separate access from the construct

Assessment is where inclusive design becomes most consequential. If learners can access instruction in multiple ways but are tested through a single irrelevant barrier, the flexibility disappears at the moment that matters most.

We should ask what evidence would convince us that the learner can perform. Sometimes that evidence should be standardized because the real world demands a standardized behavior. Other times we have room to vary the response mode while holding the criterion constant.

A scenario can allow keyboard and touch. A written analysis can be compatible with screen readers. A spoken assessment can include captions for the prompts. Time limits should exist because the performance requires speed, not because the authoring tool defaults to a timer.

AI can personalize access, but personalization needs boundaries

AI systems can translate, summarize, rephrase, generate examples, create audio, describe images, and provide conversational support. Those capabilities can expand access. They can also introduce errors, bias, privacy concerns, and inconsistency.

The design response is not to reject personalization or trust it blindly. Establish the nonnegotiable learning goal, define what the system may adapt, validate high-stakes outputs, give learners a way to recover from bad assistance, and protect sensitive information.

AI should not silently change the construct. If the learning goal is to independently write an argument, an AI system that writes the argument for the learner has crossed from access support into task replacement. If the goal is to evaluate arguments, generating multiple examples may be useful.

A practical inclusive-design review

  1. Name the construct. What capability are we actually trying to develop or measure?
  2. Identify predictable barriers. Sensory, motor, language, cognitive, cultural, technological, environmental.
  3. Remove irrelevant friction. Do not make the learner prove abilities the goal does not require.
  4. Offer meaningful options. Use flexibility when it preserves the same essential outcome.
  5. Check WCAG behavior. Keyboard, focus, contrast, labels, timing, motion, captions, errors.
  6. Test with real people. Automated checkers cannot tell you whether the experience makes sense.
  7. Protect rigor. Accessibility should expand the route to the challenge, not erase the challenge.

The design is stronger when fewer learners need exceptions

I do not expect one design to eliminate every need for accommodation. Human variability is too broad for that. The better goal is to stop creating barriers we can predict and prevent.

When a learning experience is clear, flexible, accessible, and demanding in the right places, we are not designing for a smaller group. We are designing more precisely for reality.

That is what makes inclusive design rigorous. It asks us to know exactly what matters, then stop confusing everything else with learning.

Replace the average persona with a conditions map

Traditional personas can be useful, but they become dangerous when a fictional profile is treated as a normal user. Instead of designing for “Alex, age 34, busy manager,” map the conditions under which people will learn. Ten-minute windows or uninterrupted hour? Phone or desktop? New to the language or fluent? High prior knowledge or novice? Quiet office or shared environment? Assistive technology? High consequence or casual exploration?

Conditions reveal design constraints without pretending one profile represents everyone.

Separate permanent, temporary, and situational barriers

Inclusive design becomes stronger when we remember that barriers are not limited to permanent disability. A person may have a permanent hearing loss, a temporary ear infection, or simply be watching a video in a quiet room where audio cannot play. Captions can support all three.

A person may have a motor disability, a temporary injury, or one hand occupied. Larger targets and keyboard support can help across those situations. Designing for edge conditions often improves the center.

Language accessibility is more than translation

Translation matters, but clarity begins before translation. Shorter sentences, defined technical terms, concrete examples, consistent labels, and meaningful headings help multilingual learners and native speakers alike.

Do not confuse professional language with inflated language. Expertise often allows a writer to be more precise with fewer words. If a complex term is necessary, teach it. If it is not necessary, remove it.

UDL 3.0 asks designers to consider identity, belonging, and bias

CAST’s 3.0 update places stronger emphasis on barriers rooted in bias and systems of exclusion. That expands the conversation beyond format. A technically accessible learning experience can still communicate that some identities are peripheral, examples are stereotyped, or only one way of knowing is legitimate.

Representation should not become a superficial checklist. The deeper question is whether learners can enter the experience without first translating themselves into the designer’s assumed norm.

Optimize challenge and support together

Too little challenge produces boredom. Too much produces overload. Inclusive design is often about matching support to challenge while keeping the target capability stable.

For a novice, provide a worked example and cues. For an intermediate learner, remove some cues. For an expert, introduce ambiguity and time pressure. The same outcome can be approached through different scaffolding.

Beware of accessibility changes that accidentally change the construct

If the goal is listening comprehension, replacing audio with a transcript changes what is being assessed. If the goal is historical reasoning, allowing text-to-speech may preserve the construct. Designers need to distinguish access features from construct alteration.

This is why the performance definition comes first. We cannot decide what flexibility is appropriate until we know what capability matters.

Build accessible components once and reuse them

One practical way to scale inclusive design is a small design system: accessible buttons, dialogs, tabs, cards, tables, focus states, captions, error messages, and responsive layouts that have already been tested. Designers can spend more time on learning logic and less time rediscovering basic accessibility requirements.

The shared navigation and footer across wdw.llc follow this principle. Consistency reduces cognitive load and makes future pages easier to improve centrally.

The business case is real, but dignity is enough

Accessible products can reach more people, reduce rework, improve usability, and support compliance. Those are legitimate business reasons. They should not be the only reasons.

If learning exists to increase capability, people should not have to fight unnecessary barriers simply to enter the experience. That is a quality principle before it is a legal one.

Design for the range, not the midpoint

The mythical average learner makes design feel simpler because one route appears sufficient. Real learners are more interesting and more demanding. They arrive with different histories, bodies, devices, languages, confidence, expertise, and circumstances.

We cannot anticipate everything. We can become much better at anticipating variability. That is the difference between accommodation as repair and inclusive design as architecture.

Executive-function demands are often hidden in course design

A learner may understand the content and still struggle with an experience that requires remembering several instructions, tracking multiple tabs, planning a long project, estimating time, and navigating inconsistent controls. Those are real demands, but they may not be the capability being taught.

Chunk complex instructions, keep navigation stable, make progress visible, provide checklists for multi-step work, and let learners return after interruption without reconstructing the entire state. Those supports reduce unnecessary executive load without lowering the intellectual challenge.

Design important information so one sense is not the only gate

If meaning is carried only by color, sound, animation, or visual position, some learners lose the information entirely. Use redundant cues when the construct allows it: text plus icon, caption plus audio, label plus color, description plus diagram.

Redundancy here is not the same as presenting identical information in several competing formats. The goal is to preserve access to meaning, not flood the learner with channels.

Choice needs architecture

UDL can be misread as “give everyone lots of options.” Unstructured choice can create another barrier. Good choice architecture tells learners why an option exists and keeps the core goal clear.

For example: “Read the case or listen to the narrated version. Both contain the same evidence. Then choose one of two practice contexts.” The flexibility is meaningful and bounded.

Examples should vary in more than names and photographs

Inclusive representation is not achieved by swapping character images while every scenario assumes the same family structure, workplace norm, communication style, economic condition, or cultural reference point.

Review examples for hidden assumptions. Ask whether the context is necessary to the learning goal and whether learners from different backgrounds can enter the problem without decoding avoidable cultural trivia.

Use learner data to find barriers, not to label people permanently

Analytics can reveal where users abandon, repeat, or struggle. Treat those patterns as signals about the interaction and conditions before concluding that a group “cannot” do something.

Design changes should remain testable. If larger targets reduce mobile errors, the problem may have been interface precision rather than learner ability.

A flexible system can still feel coherent

The strongest inclusive designs do not feel like twenty separate accommodations bolted onto one course. They feel like one clear experience with thoughtful routes, visible structure, and controls that respect human difference.

That is the design challenge: flexibility without fragmentation.
