Accessibility Is a Design Decision

A practical guide to building eLearning that people can actually perceive, operate, understand, and use across keyboard, mobile, audio, captions, motion, and assistive technology.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…
Access is architecturePerceive · operate · understand · use
Key takeawayAccessibility is not a cleanup task at the end of development. It is part of the interaction architecture, media plan, assessment design, and definition of quality from the beginning.

Accessibility should be visible in the design long before QA

Accessibility is often treated as a technical pass at the end of production. Run the checker. Add alt text. Fix contrast. Turn on captions. That approach can catch issues, but it cannot repair a learning experience whose core interaction was inaccessible from the beginning.

If the course depends on dragging tiny objects, if instructions exist only inside an image, if the scenario relies on color alone, or if keyboard focus jumps unpredictably through layers, the problem is architectural.

Accessibility is a design decision because every interface makes assumptions about bodies, senses, devices, language, attention, and input. Better design makes fewer unnecessary assumptions.

WCAG gives us testable behavior

WCAG 2.2 is the current W3C recommendation for accessible web content. It addresses perception, operation, understanding, and robustness across a wide range of disabilities and devices. For eLearning teams, WCAG provides a concrete baseline even when the authoring tool abstracts away the underlying HTML.

That baseline includes familiar practices such as text alternatives, captions, sufficient contrast, keyboard operability, visible focus, understandable labels, error identification, and controls for certain motion and timing.

The designer does not need to become a browser engineer to care about these behaviors. We do need to know enough to design interactions that can satisfy them.

Keyboard access is a design test, not only an accommodation

Try to complete the experience without touching the mouse. Can you reach every interactive element? Can you see where focus is? Is the order logical? Can you activate controls and close dialogs? Does focus disappear behind an overlay?

Keyboard testing often reveals general usability problems. If focus order makes no sense, the visual structure may also be unclear. If a control has no accessible name, its purpose may be ambiguous to everyone.

Captions and transcripts are learning resources

Captions support learners who are deaf or hard of hearing, but their usefulness is broader. They help in noisy environments, support unfamiliar terminology, make search and review easier, and can assist people processing a second language.

Transcripts create another study surface. Learners can scan, annotate, search, and return to a specific idea without scrubbing through video.

Good captions should reflect meaning, speakers, and relevant non-speech audio. Auto-captioning can accelerate production, but it still needs review where accuracy matters.

Audio should have an equivalent path when audio is not the construct

Audio can add emotion, accessibility, pronunciation, story, and human presence. It should not become the only route to essential information unless listening itself is what we are measuring.

If a scenario includes a customer call, provide a transcript or equivalent text representation while preserving the spoken-performance goal as appropriate. If background music is optional, let the learner control it. The music icon at the top of wdw.llc articles is intentionally user-controlled for that reason. A visitor can create a mellow reading environment or leave the page quiet.

Alt text is not a caption-writing contest

Alternative text should communicate the function or meaning the image contributes in context. Decorative images may need no verbal equivalent. A chart needs the important relationship, not a list of every pixel. An instructional diagram may need a concise description plus a longer explanation nearby.

Ask what a learner needs to know if they cannot see the image. That question is more useful than mechanically describing appearance.

Color can reinforce meaning. It should not carry meaning alone.

Red and green status indicators are common. So are blue links, highlighted correct answers, and color-coded charts. Pair color with labels, patterns, icons, text, or position so meaning survives when colors are not perceived as expected.

Contrast also matters for readability, but contrast ratios are not a substitute for hierarchy. A page can technically pass and still be visually exhausting.

Motion needs purpose and control

Animation can explain change, direct attention, and bring a concept to life. It can also trigger discomfort, distract attention, or make a page difficult to follow. Respect reduced-motion preferences where possible. Avoid unnecessary movement around reading content. Give learners control over media that plays or moves.

The SVG banners on wdw.llc use subtle motion because the animation supports identity and metaphor. The article itself should remain readable when motion is reduced or removed.

Always ask what happens when drag-and-drop is unavailable

Drag interactions are popular because they feel physical. They are also frequently difficult on mobile, keyboard, switch devices, and some assistive technologies. If the learning goal is sorting, there are other ways to let the learner classify.

Provide selectable alternatives, buttons, lists, or another equivalent interaction. Better yet, decide whether dragging adds instructional meaning or merely visual novelty.

Time limits should come from the performance, not the template

Some tasks genuinely require speed. Emergency response, fluent recall, certain production work, and timed decision environments may make time part of competence. Many learning activities do not.

When time is not the construct, rigid limits can create barriers without improving evidence. Let learners pause, resume, replay, and take the time they need where the real performance allows it.

Semantic structure is invisible design with visible benefits

Real headings, lists, labels, landmarks, and table headers help assistive technologies interpret a page. They also improve consistency, search, scanning, and maintainability.

When designers treat text simply as objects placed on a canvas, structure can disappear. The course still looks organized visually while becoming flat to a screen reader. Use the authoring tool’s structural features rather than simulating structure with font size alone.

Accessibility and UDL overlap, but they are not identical

Accessibility establishes important requirements for access. UDL asks broader questions about learner variability, agency, representation, engagement, and action. A course can technically meet accessibility criteria and still be instructionally inflexible. It can also offer many learning choices while failing basic accessibility.

The strongest practice uses both: standards to protect access and instructional design to create meaningful learning.

Automated checkers cannot tell you whether the experience makes sense

Automated tools are useful for detecting certain failures. They cannot determine whether alt text communicates the right idea, whether a keyboard sequence is cognitively logical, whether captions support comprehension, or whether the experience becomes exhausting with assistive technology.

Whenever possible, include people with disabilities in testing and design feedback. Accessibility improves when it is informed by actual use rather than abstract compliance.

Accessibility is also a quality and business decision

Accessible design expands reach, reduces rework, supports legal and policy obligations, and often improves the experience for everyone. More importantly, it communicates who the product assumes belongs.

Learning products are supposed to increase capability. It is contradictory to create a capability-building experience that people cannot enter or operate.

A practical eLearning accessibility pass

  1. Complete the experience by keyboard.
  2. Check visible focus and logical order.
  3. Review headings, labels, and link purpose.
  4. Verify color contrast and non-color cues.
  5. Caption meaningful audio and provide transcripts when useful.
  6. Review alt text for purpose, not decoration.
  7. Provide alternatives to precision drag or hover-only interaction.
  8. Respect zoom, mobile, and reduced-motion settings.
  9. Check timing and pause controls.
  10. Test with real users and assistive technology when possible.

Accessibility is part of whether the learning works

I do not see accessibility as a layer placed over instructional design. It is part of instructional design because access determines whether the learner can perceive the information, operate the experience, practice the skill, and demonstrate the outcome.

When accessibility is designed in from the beginning, the course becomes easier to maintain, easier to use, and more honest about the range of people we claim to serve.

Use interaction patterns people already know

Custom controls can look distinctive and become difficult for keyboard users, screen readers, mobile users, and anyone who relies on familiar behavior. Native buttons, links, checkboxes, headings, and dialogs carry expectations that browsers and assistive technologies understand.

Innovation belongs in the learning experience more than in reinventing basic controls.

Focus management matters when interfaces change

Dialogs, popups, branching panels, and dynamically revealed content can leave keyboard or screen-reader focus in the wrong place. When a modal opens, focus should move into it. When it closes, focus should return sensibly. When content updates, the user may need a clear indication of what changed.

These details are invisible to many designers and essential to some learners.

Forms need useful errors, not just red borders

If a learner misses a required field, say what happened and how to fix it. Do not rely on color alone. Preserve entered information when possible. Put errors close to the relevant field and provide a summary when the form is complex.

Error recovery is part of accessibility because people should not have to restart a task because the interface explained failure poorly.

Target size is a learning issue on touch screens

Small controls create accidental taps and motor precision demands unrelated to most learning goals. WCAG 2.2 added criteria related to target size and focus behavior that reflect modern interaction realities.

On mobile, generous targets and spacing improve the experience for everyone, especially when a learner is moving, tired, using one hand, or has limited dexterity.

Downloads need accessibility too

An accessible webpage can link to an inaccessible PDF, slide deck, or worksheet and break the path. If downloadable materials are essential, headings, reading order, alt text, table structure, and document tags matter there as well.

Accessibility follows the learner across formats.

Language metadata and plain language support assistive technology

Pages and passages should identify their language so screen readers can pronounce text appropriately. Clear language, meaningful headings, and descriptive links also reduce cognitive burden.

“Click here” tells a screen-reader user very little when links are navigated out of context. “Read the WCAG 2.2 overview” is much more useful.

Accessibility belongs in tool selection

If an authoring platform cannot produce accessible interactions, the problem cannot be fixed entirely by designer effort. Ask vendors about keyboard support, semantic output, captions, focus, responsive behavior, and accessibility documentation before the team is locked into production.

Tool capability is part of the design environment.

The goal is not perfect compliance. It is fewer preventable barriers.

Standards matter because they create shared, testable expectations. Real accessibility also requires judgment, testing, and humility. No checklist captures every human experience.

Design early, test often, listen to users, and treat access as part of whether the learning works at all.

Accessibility changes how we design scenarios

A branching scenario is a good stress test because it combines reading, navigation, decisions, feedback, and sometimes audio or video. If a learner must use a mouse to reveal a choice, if the choices are embedded in an image, or if feedback disappears before it can be reviewed, the experience is asking for abilities that may have nothing to do with the learning goal.

I like to separate the performance demand from the interface demand. If the learner is supposed to decide how to respond to a difficult conversation, then the hard part should be interpreting the situation and choosing a response. The hard part should not be finding a tiny hotspot or guessing which element has keyboard focus.

This distinction makes accessibility a design discipline rather than a compliance chore. We preserve the cognitive challenge while reducing irrelevant physical, sensory, or navigational barriers.

Plan media alternatives before production starts

Media becomes expensive to retrofit because the alternative often depends on decisions made during scripting. A video that communicates critical information only through a visual gesture may need more than captions. An animation that shows a process with no narration may need a description. A podcast-like segment may need a transcript that preserves speakers and important sound cues.

A simple media plan can prevent much of the rework. For every media object, identify the instructional purpose, the information channel it uses, and the equivalent route a learner needs if that channel is unavailable. That does not mean every format must be identical. It means essential meaning and required action remain available.

AI can accelerate accessibility work, but it cannot own the final decision

AI tools can generate captions, transcripts, image descriptions, translations, summaries, and alternate formats quickly. That can remove hours of mechanical work. It also creates a new quality problem: generated accessibility content can be fluent and still be wrong.

A transcript can mishear a technical term. An image description can describe objects while missing the instructional relationship. A translation can preserve grammar and lose domain meaning. A generated alt text can be too long because the model does not know what the learner needs from the image.

The productive workflow is acceleration plus review. Let the tool produce the first pass. Let the designer or subject matter expert decide whether the result communicates the right meaning. Accessibility is not simply having an alternative. It is having an alternative that does the job.

Mobile exposes assumptions desktop design can hide

A course that looks comfortable on a 27-inch monitor can become a precision test on a phone. Hover states disappear. Two-column layouts collapse. Small targets become harder to tap. Long labels wrap. Popups cover the content they are supposed to explain.

Testing on mobile is therefore not only responsive QA. It is a way of discovering whether the interaction depends on generous screen space, fine motor control, or visual memory across distant areas of the page.

When the learning goal permits it, I prefer interactions that survive changing devices without changing the underlying thinking. Select instead of drag. Expand instead of hover. Use one clear action per control. Let the interface get simpler as the available space gets smaller.

Accessibility needs an owner at every handoff

One of the easiest ways for accessibility to disappear is to make it everyone's responsibility in theory and no one's responsibility in practice. The designer assumes development will fix it. Development assumes QA will catch it. QA identifies a problem after the deadline. The problem is then downgraded because changing it would affect the schedule.

A better workflow makes accessibility visible in acceptance criteria. The script identifies media alternatives. The design specifies focus behavior and interaction alternatives. Development implements them. QA tests them. The release decision treats unresolved barriers like other quality defects rather than optional polish.

That is the larger principle: accessibility improves when it is built into the definition of done.

Accessibility belongs in the first sketch

Accessibility becomes expensive when it is treated as a repair step. If captions, keyboard behavior, reading order, contrast, language complexity, and alternative formats are considered only after development, every fix competes with a design decision that has already hardened.

The better workflow is to include accessibility in the first wireframe and prototype. Decide how someone will navigate without a mouse. Decide what a visual means if it cannot be seen. Decide what an audio experience communicates if it cannot be heard. Decide whether movement is essential or decorative. Decide how errors will be identified without relying on color alone.

Wayne’s field noteUDL taught me to see variability as normal, not exceptional. Accessibility takes that same respect and gives it technical and ethical discipline. The goal is not to create a special version for “other” people. The goal is to design a strong experience that anticipates more ways of perceiving, navigating, and understanding from the beginning.

Test the path, not only the page

A page can pass individual checks and still create an inaccessible experience across a sequence. Test the full path. Can someone reach the menu, understand the current location, operate the interaction, recover from an error, move to the next section, and complete the assessment without getting trapped?

That is particularly important in branching scenarios, custom HTML, and interactive tools. Accessibility is experienced over time.

Clarity is an accessibility feature

Clear language supports people with different reading levels, cognitive loads, language backgrounds, and attention conditions. It also helps experts who are tired, rushed, or using a small screen. Plain language does not mean making the thinking simplistic. It means removing avoidable friction between the idea and the person trying to use it.

Keep specialized language where precision requires it. Define it when the audience may not share the vocabulary. Break long instructions into actions. Put important conditions near the decision they affect.

Accessibility is also a quality signal

When a portfolio demonstrates keyboard access, meaningful structure, readable contrast, reduced-motion support, captions, and thoughtful alternatives, it communicates more than compliance. It shows that the designer has considered people beyond the ideal user on the ideal device under ideal conditions.

That is the standard I would want attached to the work: inclusive by design, testable in practice, and treated as part of professional quality rather than a decorative checklist at the end.
