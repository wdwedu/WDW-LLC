Ancient Wisdom for the AI Age

What Stoicism, Ubuntu, Ma’at, and other philosophical traditions can contribute to agency, responsibility, identity, and meaning in a machine-mediated world.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202620 min readShare

Old questions · new machinesAgency · virtue · relation
Key takeawayThe most difficult problems created by powerful technology are not only technical. They are questions of character, judgment, responsibility, relationship, and meaning. Ancient traditions cannot tell us how to regulate a neural network or benchmark an autonomous agent, but they can sharpen the human capacities we will need while those systems become more capable.

Why ancient philosophy becomes more relevant when technology accelerates

Every technological transition changes more than tools. It changes what people expect from themselves, from one another, and from institutions. Artificial intelligence is already changing how we write, search, design, learn, analyze, create, hire, teach, sell, and make decisions. As systems become more capable, the temptation is to treat every human problem as an optimization problem: find the fastest output, remove friction, automate the repeated step, predict the next action, and delegate the rest.

Efficiency matters. But a life cannot be reduced to throughput. A school cannot be judged only by the speed at which it generates content. A company cannot be judged only by output per employee. A person cannot be measured only by how many tasks can be completed with an assistant model. Human beings still have to decide what deserves attention, what should not be automated, which obligations matter, what counts as enough, and how to live with uncertainty when predictions remain imperfect.

That is where philosophy matters. Not as decoration. Not as a collection of inspirational quotations. Philosophy is practice for deciding what is worth doing when the answer is not supplied by a tool.

WDW lensThe AI age raises an instructional-design question as much as a philosophical one: if technology can increasingly perform the visible task, what should education develop in the person? One answer is deeper judgment—reasoning, ethical discernment, self-regulation, perspective-taking, transfer, and the ability to remain responsible for decisions made with powerful tools.

Four traditions, four different questions

It is important not to collapse distinct traditions into one generic category called “ancient wisdom.” Stoicism emerged from the Greek and Roman world. Ma’at belongs to ancient Egyptian religious, moral, and political thought. Ubuntu is a family of southern African ideas developed and interpreted across different languages, communities, and modern philosophical debates. Aristotelian virtue ethics comes from a different intellectual lineage again. They overlap at points, but they are not interchangeable.

The useful approach is comparative: ask what problem each tradition places in the foreground.

Stoicism: protect the part of the process that is still yours

One of the most useful Stoic distinctions is between what depends on us and what does not. Epictetus begins the Enchiridion by separating our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions from external events that are not fully ours to command. That distinction can sound almost too simple until technology begins producing more external volatility than a person can reasonably track.

You cannot control whether a new model releases next month. You cannot control whether an employer adopts automation. You cannot control whether a platform changes its algorithm or whether a profession reorganizes around new tools. You can control whether you learn deliberately, whether you verify what a model tells you, whether you cultivate transferable skill, whether you make your work legible, and whether you allow constant technological novelty to fracture your attention.

In that sense, Stoicism offers an antidote to AI panic and AI intoxication at the same time. Panic gives external change too much authority over the mind. Intoxication gives the tool too much authority over judgment. The Stoic response is not “ignore technology.” It is “locate responsibility accurately.”

A Stoic operating questionBefore using AI, ask: What part of this decision still belongs to me?

If the answer is accuracy, values, consequences, interpretation, or accountability, that portion should not disappear simply because a system can generate a fluent answer.

Ubuntu: intelligence without relationship is incomplete

Ubuntu is often translated through phrases such as “a person is a person through other persons,” but scholars have emphasized that there is no single uncontested definition. In broad terms, Ubuntu-oriented thought treats personhood as relational rather than radically individual. Human flourishing is connected to community, reciprocity, dignity, and the ways people recognize one another.

That matters in a technological culture that increasingly personalizes everything. AI systems can individualize tutoring, recommendations, workflows, feeds, assistants, and even companionship. Personalization can be useful. But if every friction is removed between the individual and the community, we can also remove some of the places where people practice patience, obligation, negotiation, care, and shared meaning.

For education and workplace learning, an Ubuntu lens asks whether technology is strengthening participation or quietly isolating the learner. Does the system help a novice enter a community of practice? Does it make expertise more shareable? Does it help people see the consequences of their actions on others? Or does it replace every conversation with an interface?

The question is not whether AI can simulate a supportive response. The deeper question is whether the system strengthens real human relationships and shared capability.

Ma’at: truth is not merely information

Ma’at is commonly associated with truth, justice, balance, order, and rightness in ancient Egyptian thought. It was not simply a personal virtue. It also described an ideal of cosmic and social order, tied to legitimate rule, moral conduct, and the maintenance of a world that should not fall into disorder.

That makes Ma’at a useful philosophical contrast to the information abundance of the AI era. A system can generate more information than a person can evaluate. It can produce competing explanations, images, summaries, arguments, and simulated evidence. Quantity does not establish truth. Fluency does not establish right order.

A Ma’at-inspired question is therefore broader than “Is this technically accurate?” It asks whether the information is being used in a way that supports truthfulness, justice, proportion, and responsible order. In a workplace, a prediction may be statistically useful and still be deployed unfairly. In education, personalized recommendations may increase efficiency while hiding unequal assumptions. In public life, synthetic media may be technically impressive while degrading the conditions required for shared reality.

Design implicationAI literacy should include epistemic responsibility: where a claim came from, how confident we should be, what evidence would change the conclusion, who may be harmed by an error, and who remains accountable when the model is wrong.

Virtue ethics: the repeated shortcut becomes a habit

Aristotle’s account of virtue emphasizes habituation. We become a certain kind of person through repeated action, guided by practical wisdom. That perspective becomes especially useful when AI can remove effort from almost any cognitive task.

Effort is not automatically valuable. Nobody needs to perform a tedious calculation by hand simply to prove seriousness. But some forms of effort are developmental. Drafting an argument can reveal whether we understand it. Retrieving information from memory strengthens access to it. Struggling with a decision can expose competing values. Revising a weak explanation can sharpen judgment.

The educational danger is not that students or professionals use AI. The danger is that they repeatedly outsource the exact mental activity they are supposed to be developing. If the learner delegates interpretation before learning how to interpret, the shortcut becomes a substitute for capability.

This is why “AI-resistant assignments” are only a partial answer. A more durable question is: which parts of the task are developmental, and which parts are merely procedural? Automate the procedural burden when appropriate. Protect the developmental struggle.

A five-part human agency model for machine-assisted work

Across these traditions, a shared practical theme emerges: human agency is not simply the ability to choose from options. It includes the ability to understand what is happening, judge what matters, act intentionally, remain connected to others, and accept responsibility for consequences.

Attention.Can I decide what deserves focus, or is my attention being continuously captured by novelty, alerts, recommendations, and generated possibilities?
Judgment.Can I evaluate an answer rather than merely accept it because it is fast, confident, or convenient?
Purpose.Do I know why the task matters before optimizing how quickly it can be completed?
Relationship.Does the technology strengthen my obligations and participation with other people, or replace them by default?
Accountability.If the result harms someone or fails, can I explain the decision and own my part in it?

What should education develop when machines can produce answers?

For decades, schools and training organizations have often treated visible production as evidence of learning. Write the report. Complete the worksheet. Build the presentation. Submit the analysis. AI makes that assumption increasingly fragile because the artifact can now be generated without the underlying capability being present.

The response should not be nostalgia for a world without tools. Calculators did not eliminate mathematics. Search engines did not eliminate knowledge. Word processors did not eliminate writing. But every tool changes which evidence remains trustworthy.

In an AI-rich environment, education needs stronger demonstrations of reasoning: oral explanation, live decision-making, critique of competing outputs, transfer to unfamiliar situations, defense of choices, reflection on evidence, collaborative problem solving, and iterative revision. The product still matters. The process becomes more visible.

This is where philosophy and instructional design meet. A curriculum is always making an implicit claim about the kind of person it hopes to develop. If that claim is only “a person who can produce correct-looking output,” AI will outperform the curriculum. If the goal includes judgment, agency, ethical reasoning, adaptability, and participation in human communities, education has deeper work to do.

What this means for work and leadership

Organizations face the same issue. A company can use AI to increase output while quietly weakening expertise. If junior workers no longer perform the early-stage work through which experts historically learned the profession, where will future experts come from? If managers rely on generated summaries rather than direct observation, what happens to context? If every employee optimizes individual productivity, what happens to shared learning?

Leaders therefore need two dashboards, not one. The first measures efficiency: time saved, cost reduced, throughput increased. The second measures capability: judgment retained, expertise developed, errors caught, collaboration improved, knowledge transferred, and decisions explained.

How philosophy helps us cope with uncertainty about AGI

Predictions about artificial general intelligence vary widely. Some observers expect rapid capability gains; others argue that benchmark progress can be misleading, that deployment constraints matter, and that “general intelligence” itself is difficult to define. Philosophy does not resolve that empirical debate. It does something different: it prepares the person to live without certainty.

Stoicism says we can act well without controlling the future. Ubuntu reminds us that adaptation should not become solitary survival. Ma’at reminds us that truth, justice, and order matter even when information becomes cheap. Virtue ethics reminds us that repeated behavior forms character, including repeated dependence on machines.

That combination produces a practical stance neither fearful nor naive: learn the tools, study the evidence, prepare for change, protect human agency, strengthen relationships, and refuse to confuse capability with wisdom.

A weekly practice for staying human-directed

Philosophy becomes useful when it becomes behavioral. Once a week, review one important AI-assisted decision and ask five questions:

  1. Control: What part of the outcome did I actually control?
  2. Truth: What did I verify rather than merely accept?
  3. Character: What habit did this workflow strengthen in me?
  4. Relationship: Who else was affected, included, excluded, or made more capable?
  5. Responsibility: Could I explain and defend the final decision without blaming the tool?

Those five questions take less time than most prompt-engineering rituals, yet they may be more important for long-term professional judgment.

The future is not only something that happens to us

Technology changes the conditions of action, but conditions are not destiny. The central human question remains what it has always been: given the world we actually inhabit, what kind of person and community should we become?

The AI age makes that question harder because machines increasingly participate in activities we once used to define intelligence, creativity, expertise, and even companionship. That can feel destabilizing. It can also clarify what should never have depended on exclusivity in the first place. Human worth was never supposed to rest on being the only creature that could draft a memo, generate an image, summarize a book, or solve a puzzle.

Our harder work is judgment. Responsibility. Meaning. Relationship. Character. The ability to decide what should be done when a machine can tell us what could be done.

Ancient philosophy cannot give us a technical roadmap for AGI. It can give us something a roadmap cannot: disciplines for remaining human-directed while the map keeps changing.

Frequently asked questions

Is Stoicism anti-emotion?

No. Classical Stoic practice is concerned with judgments, passions, and living according to reason and virtue. Modern popular versions sometimes reduce Stoicism to emotional suppression, which is a distortion.

Is Ubuntu one single ancient doctrine?

No. Ubuntu is better understood as a family of related African ethical and philosophical ideas with different formulations and modern interpretations. It should not be treated as a single frozen slogan.

Why include Ma’at in a discussion of technology?

Because AI creates epistemic and institutional problems as well as technical ones. A tradition concerned with truth, order, justice, and balance provides a useful comparative lens for thinking about how information and power should be used.

Does philosophy slow innovation?

Good philosophy slows only the decisions that deserve reflection. It can also accelerate clarity by exposing assumptions, values, and tradeoffs before organizations invest heavily in the wrong solution.
