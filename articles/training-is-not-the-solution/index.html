Performance + Curriculum

Training Is Not the Solution Until You Prove It Is

Why strong learning teams diagnose systems, incentives, tools, expectations, and opportunity before prescribing another course.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202615 min read
Diagnose before prescribingTraining fixes only some gaps
Key takeawayA performance problem is not automatically a knowledge problem. Training is appropriate when people need knowledge, skill, practice, or confidence that instruction can realistically change. If the root cause is unclear expectations, broken tools, missing resources, conflicting incentives, or impossible workload, another course can become an expensive way to avoid the real problem.

The order-taking trap

Learning teams are often contacted after the solution has already been named: “We need a course.” That request can come from a real pain point, but the proposed intervention may be premature. A designer who immediately starts storyboarding is accepting the client’s diagnosis without examination.

Performance consulting begins one step earlier. What is happening now? What should be happening? Who is affected? What evidence shows the gap? Under what conditions does performance improve or deteriorate? What changed before the problem appeared? Those questions do not delay design; they protect it from solving the wrong problem.

Five families of performance causes

Expectations and feedback.Do people know what good performance looks like, why it matters, and how they are doing?
Tools and environment.Do systems, time, staffing, access, and workflow support the expected behavior?
Consequences and incentives.What gets rewarded, ignored, or discouraged in practice?
Knowledge and skill.Can people perform the task, recognize the cues, and make the required decisions?
Capacity and role fit.Are the task, authority, workload, and individual capabilities realistically aligned?

Training has strong leverage in the knowledge-and-skill category and sometimes supports expectations or confidence. It has weak leverage over a broken approval process, missing permissions, or a manager who rewards speed while policy requires careful review.

Evidence before content

Before collecting content, collect performance evidence. Interview high and average performers separately. Observe the task if possible. Review error logs, support tickets, audit findings, customer complaints, quality scores, cycle time, rework, and manager feedback. Compare conditions where the task succeeds with conditions where it fails.

The goal is not to build a perfect causal model. It is to become confident enough to choose an intervention that can plausibly change the outcome. If the evidence is thin, say so. A small pilot and measurement plan may be more responsible than a full-scale learning build.

When training is appropriate

Training is appropriate when the performer lacks knowledge, skill, mental models, cue recognition, decision practice, or confidence that can be improved through instruction and practice. It is especially valuable when errors are costly, opportunities to practice on the job are rare, or feedback in the work environment arrives too late.

Strong training also acknowledges external supports. If experts use a checklist, reference, or calculator, training should teach correct use of that tool rather than forcing unnecessary memorization. The learning target is authentic performance, not maximal recall.

Why solutions are often blended

Real performance gaps are frequently multicausal. A new employee may need training while experienced employees need a clearer job aid. Managers may need coaching. The process may need one approval removed. The software may need a better error message. A blended intervention is not a compromise; it can be the most accurate response to the evidence.

Design principleUse training for what training can change. Use systems, communication, process, tools, incentives, and leadership interventions for the rest.

Worked example: “Employees are not documenting customer calls”

The initial request is a 30-minute refresher course. Investigation shows that employees know the policy and can demonstrate it in a test environment. Observation reveals that the documentation screen takes time to load, supervisors pressure teams to reduce average handle time, and the required fields are poorly aligned to the conversation.

A course may remind people of the rule, but it does not remove the conflict. The stronger solution might include a streamlined form, adjusted productivity metrics, a short job aid for edge cases, manager messaging, and targeted practice only for the parts employees actually misunderstand.

This example also shows why designers can create business value before authoring anything. Diagnosing accurately can save development time, reduce seat time, and improve the chance that performance actually changes.

Portfolio lensA senior-level case study should show the diagnosis that preceded the deliverable. Demonstrating that you prevented unnecessary training can be as impressive as demonstrating that you built excellent training.

The training request is often a symptom

Learning teams frequently receive requests after a problem has already been interpreted. “We need a refresher.” “Build onboarding.” “People need communication training.” The request arrives with the solution attached, which makes it easy to skip diagnosis.

A strong designer treats the request as a hypothesis. What behavior is not happening? How often? Who performs it well? What changed? What happens immediately before the error? What consequence follows the desired behavior? What tools, time, permissions, and information are available?

These questions are not bureaucracy. They are protection against spending money on a course that cannot affect the cause.

When training really is the right answer

Training is appropriate when people need knowledge, skill, mental models, or practice they do not currently possess and when the environment will allow them to use what they learn. New systems, new procedures, new decision standards, new products, and genuinely new roles often require learning.

Training is also useful when performance is inconsistent because people have not had enough deliberate practice. In those cases, information alone is not enough. The intervention needs examples, guided attempts, feedback, increasing difficulty, and realistic assessment.

The key is that the learning need can be named. “They need to know the escalation criteria and practice applying them to ambiguous cases” is a training problem. “They know the criteria but the system hides the escalation button” is not.

How to say “not training” without becoming the department of no

The most effective response is not rejection. It is reframing. “We may need training, but before we build it, let’s confirm what is preventing the behavior.” Then return with evidence and an intervention mix.

Stakeholders are more receptive when the learning team connects the diagnosis to speed, cost, risk, and measurable performance. The goal is not to prove that training is unnecessary. The goal is to solve the business problem with the smallest effective combination of interventions.

This approach also increases trust. When the learning team recommends training only when it can plausibly work, stakeholders learn that a course recommendation carries analytical weight.

If training is part of the solution, design the environment too

Even valid training can fail without reinforcement. Plan what learners see when they return to work. Will a job aid be available? Will managers coach the behavior? Will the system provide prompts? Will metrics reward the new process? Will learners have an opportunity to perform soon enough that the knowledge does not decay?

Training works best when the environment expects, supports, and measures the same behavior the learning experience practiced.

The wrong intervention has opportunity cost

A weak training intervention costs more than development hours. Employees spend time away from work. Managers lose credibility when people are assigned content that does not address the real obstacle. Learning teams consume capacity that could have been used on a genuine skill gap. Repeatedly solving system problems with courses can train the organization to treat learning as a communication channel rather than a performance function.

That opportunity cost is why diagnosis matters even for small requests. The analysis can be lightweight, but it should exist.

Use the minimum effective intervention

Some problems need a course. Others need a five-minute practice, a checklist, a redesigned screen, a manager script, a clearer policy, or a searchable reference. The minimum effective intervention is the smallest combination that plausibly changes the target behavior.

This principle reduces development time and often improves adoption because the solution fits the moment of need.

Close the loop after launch

A performance intervention should create a feedback loop. Did the behavior change? Did the barrier move somewhere else? Did employees create a workaround? Did managers reinforce the new expectation? Did a metric improve while another deteriorated?

Post-launch evidence turns one project into organizational learning. It helps the team refine the intervention and improves future diagnosis because assumptions are compared with reality.

Show the diagnosis in the portfolio

For hiring managers, a case study that begins with a request and then demonstrates how the designer reframed it is powerful. Show the original request, evidence gathered, cause map, intervention choices, prototype, and measurement plan. Even when confidential data cannot be shown, the reasoning can be reconstructed with anonymized details.

This signals that the designer can operate upstream of development and contribute to business performance rather than waiting for content to be handed over.

A simple decision tree for incoming requests

When a request arrives, begin with four questions. First: is the expected behavior clear? Second: can people currently perform it under ideal conditions? Third: does the work environment allow and reinforce it? Fourth: is there evidence that practice or knowledge is missing? If the answer to the fourth question is yes after the first three are addressed, training is more likely to help.

If expectations are unclear, clarify them. If people can perform but the environment blocks them, fix the environment. If people lack the capability, design practice. This simple sequence prevents the learning team from using course development as the default response to every performance symptom.

This is where learning teams become strategic partners

The difference between a production function and a strategic learning function is not the software it uses. It is the quality of the questions asked before production starts. Teams that diagnose causes, recommend mixed interventions, and connect learning to measurable performance become more valuable to leaders because they help allocate time and money intelligently.

That contribution is especially important as AI makes content generation cheaper. When draft production becomes abundant, diagnosis and judgment become more—not less—important.

Diagnosis does not have to be slow

A lightweight diagnostic can happen in a single conversation when the stakes are modest. Ask for the target behavior, one concrete example of failure, one example of success, the major barriers, and the consequence of the gap. Then confirm whether the problem is new capability, inconsistent execution, or an environmental obstacle. The point is not to build a consulting engagement around every request. The point is to avoid committing to a solution before the cause has been considered.

For larger or riskier problems, the same questions expand into observation, data analysis, interviews, and testing. The scale of analysis should match the cost of being wrong.

Why organizations reach for training so quickly

Training is visible, purchasable, schedulable, and familiar. It gives leaders a concrete response to a problem. Process redesign, incentive changes, system fixes, staffing, and role clarity are messier because they cross organizational boundaries.

That makes training an attractive container for problems it cannot solve. The learning team should understand this pressure so it can redirect the conversation constructively rather than simply saying no.

Use a “yes, and” consulting stance

Instead of “Training will not fix this,” try: “Yes, we can support the knowledge and skill component, and the data suggests we also need to address the workflow barrier.” This keeps the learning team collaborative while protecting against false promises.

Performance consulting is partly the art of expanding the solution without making the stakeholder feel that their request was foolish.

Keep a menu of non-training interventions

BarrierPossible non-training intervention

Conflicting incentiveMetric or consequence change

The menu helps teams think in systems rather than defaulting to courses.

When training really is the answer, be unapologetically instructional

Performance consulting should not make designers afraid to recommend training. If the evidence shows a genuine knowledge or skill gap, build the strongest learning experience possible. Use retrieval, authentic practice, feedback, spacing, transfer, and realistic support.

The point is not fewer courses. The point is courses used for problems courses can solve.

Explain the business risk of a false training solution

Training a non-training problem has costs beyond development time. Employees lose trust when they are sent to courses that ignore obvious barriers. Managers conclude L&D does not understand the work. The original performance problem persists. Leaders may then order another intervention because the first one “did not stick.”

A short root-cause analysis can be cheaper than a polished program that targets the wrong cause.

AI can make the training reflex worse by making content cheap

When a team can generate a course outline, slides, narration, quiz items, and video quickly, the friction that once forced reflection disappears. That makes analysis more important. Cheap production can multiply weak interventions faster.

Use AI after the performance question is clear. Speed should accelerate the right solution, not remove the diagnosis.

The learning team earns influence by solving more than learning problems

Stakeholders begin to trust a learning function when it can say, “Here is what training can change, here is what it cannot, and here is the evidence.” That is a stronger position than simply accepting every request.

Sometimes the best thing an instructional designer can build is not a course. It is clarity.

Reframe the request without alienating the requester

When someone asks for training, the wrong response is often to tell them they do not need training. That can sound dismissive, especially when the person is responsible for solving a visible problem. A better move is to accept the concern and widen the diagnosis.

I like questions that keep the requester engaged: “What would people be doing differently if this were working?” “When does the breakdown happen?” “What do strong performers do?” “What tools or conditions make the task easier or harder?” “If everyone knew exactly what to do tomorrow, what would still stop them?” Those questions respect the original concern while revealing whether learning is actually the constraint.

Wayne’s field noteOne lesson from classrooms carries directly into organizational learning: repeating an explanation louder does not fix every performance problem. Sometimes the barrier is confidence. Sometimes it is practice. Sometimes it is time, tools, incentives, unclear expectations, or an environment that punishes the behavior we claim to want.

Build the minimum learning intervention that can work

Once learning is confirmed as part of the solution, resist the urge to build the largest course the budget allows. Build the smallest intervention that gives people the knowledge, practice, feedback, and support required for the target performance.

That might be a five-minute demonstration followed by a realistic scenario. It might be a one-page checklist plus manager observation. It might be a guided simulation for the decisions that are hardest to practice on the job. The size of the intervention should follow the complexity of the performance, not the size of the stakeholder’s anxiety.

Managers are often part of the learning environment

If people leave training and return to supervisors who reward a different behavior, the course will lose. If managers cannot recognize good performance, reinforce it, or create time to practice it, transfer becomes fragile.

For important behavior change, include the manager layer in the design. Give supervisors a short observation guide, examples of expected performance, coaching questions, and a clear signal for when escalation is needed. That is often more powerful than adding another twenty minutes to the course.

Success is not proving the course worked

The goal is not to defend the learning product. The goal is to improve the performance problem that justified the work. If the evidence shows the course was not enough, that is useful information. If a process change did more than the training, that is a success too.

Learning teams gain credibility when they are willing to say, “This part needs instruction, this part needs support at the point of work, and this part needs a system change.” The value of the designer is not measured by how many courses get built. It is measured by how well the organization can perform after the work is done.

Create a performance contract before development

Once training is confirmed as part of the solution, write a short agreement that keeps the project connected to the original problem. It does not need legal language. It can be one page. State the target behavior, the conditions under which it should occur, the most important barriers, what the learning intervention will address, what it will not address, and what evidence will be used after launch.

This protects the learning team from becoming responsible for problems outside its control while also making the team accountable for the part it can influence.

Design reinforcement before launch

Training often receives all the production attention while reinforcement is added at the end. Reverse that. Decide early what performers will see after the course: a manager prompt, a job aid, an automated reminder, a sample artifact, a peer discussion, a checklist, or a follow-up scenario. The post-training environment is part of the learning system.

If the real task happens only once a month, reinforcement may matter more than adding another module during initial training. If the task happens every day, the work environment itself can become the practice space if feedback is timely and expectations are clear.

Saying no to unnecessary training can increase trust

There is a paradox here. A learning team may fear that questioning a training request makes it look less helpful. In practice, thoughtful diagnosis often increases credibility because stakeholders see that the team is trying to solve the business or learning problem rather than maximize production.

The goal is not to build fewer things for the sake of building fewer things. It is to build the right things, at the right size, connected to the conditions that allow the learning to survive.
