Performance + Curriculum

Training Is Not the Solution Until You Prove It Is

Why strong learning teams diagnose systems, incentives, tools, expectations, and opportunity before prescribing another course.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202615 min readShare
Diagnose before prescribingTraining fixes only some gaps
Key takeawayA performance problem is not automatically a knowledge problem. Training is appropriate when people need knowledge, skill, practice, or confidence that instruction can realistically change. If the root cause is unclear expectations, broken tools, missing resources, conflicting incentives, or impossible workload, another course can become an expensive way to avoid the real problem.

The order-taking trap

Learning teams are often contacted after the solution has already been named: “We need a course.” That request can come from a real pain point, but the proposed intervention may be premature. A designer who immediately starts storyboarding is accepting the client’s diagnosis without examination.

Performance consulting begins one step earlier. What is happening now? What should be happening? Who is affected? What evidence shows the gap? Under what conditions does performance improve or deteriorate? What changed before the problem appeared? Those questions do not delay design; they protect it from solving the wrong problem.

Five families of performance causes

Performance models differ in terminology, but they converge on a practical idea: behavior is shaped by more than knowledge. A useful first pass is to investigate five families of causes.

Expectations and feedback.Do people know what good performance looks like, why it matters, and how they are doing?
Tools and environment.Do systems, time, staffing, access, and workflow support the expected behavior?
Consequences and incentives.What gets rewarded, ignored, or punished in practice?
Knowledge and skill.Can people perform the task, recognize the cues, and make the required decisions?
Capacity and role fit.Are the task, authority, workload, and individual capabilities realistically aligned?

Training has strong leverage in the fourth category and sometimes supports the first and fifth. It has weak leverage over a broken approval process, missing permissions, or a manager who rewards speed while the policy demands careful review.

Evidence before content

Before collecting content, collect performance evidence. Interview high and average performers separately. Observe the task if possible. Review error logs, support tickets, audit findings, customer complaints, quality scores, cycle time, rework, and manager feedback. Compare conditions where the task succeeds with conditions where it fails.

The goal is not to build a perfect causal model. It is to become confident enough to choose an intervention that can plausibly change the outcome. If the evidence is thin, say so. A small pilot and measurement plan may be more responsible than a full-scale learning build.

When training is appropriate

Training is appropriate when the performer lacks knowledge, skill, mental models, cue recognition, decision practice, or confidence that can be improved through instruction and practice. It is especially valuable when errors are costly, opportunities to practice on the job are rare, or feedback in the work environment arrives too late.

Strong training also acknowledges external supports. If experts use a checklist, reference, or calculator, training should teach correct use of that tool rather than forcing unnecessary memorization. The learning target is authentic performance, not maximal recall.

Why solutions are often blended

Real performance gaps are frequently multicausal. A new employee may need training while experienced employees need a clearer job aid. Managers may need coaching. The process may need one approval removed. The software may need a better error message. A blended intervention is not a compromise; it can be the most accurate response to the evidence.

Design principleUse training for what training can change. Use systems, communication, process, tools, incentives, and leadership interventions for the rest.

Worked example: “Employees are not documenting customer calls”

The initial request is a 30-minute refresher course. Investigation shows that employees know the policy and can demonstrate it in a test environment. Observation reveals that the documentation screen takes forty seconds to load, supervisors pressure teams to reduce average handle time, and the required fields are poorly aligned to the conversation.

A course may remind people of the rule, but it does not remove the conflict. The stronger solution might include a streamlined form, adjusted productivity metrics, a short job aid for edge cases, manager messaging, and targeted practice only for the parts employees actually misunderstand.

This example also shows why designers can create business value before authoring anything. Diagnosing accurately can save development time, reduce seat time, and improve the chance that performance actually changes.

Portfolio lensA senior-level case study should show the diagnosis that preceded the deliverable. Demonstrating that you prevented unnecessary training can be as impressive as demonstrating that you built excellent training.
