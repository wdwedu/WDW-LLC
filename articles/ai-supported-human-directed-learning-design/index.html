AI + Automation

AI-Supported. Human-Directed.

A practical governance framework for using generative AI to accelerate learning design without outsourcing judgment, evidence, accessibility, or accountability.

Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 202614 min read
Key takeawayGenerative AI is most valuable in learning design when it increases the speed and range of human judgment—not when it replaces the judgment. The design question is not “Where can AI do the work?” but “Where can AI expand options while a responsible designer still owns the decision?”

The real AI problem is not capability. It is misplaced authority.

Generative AI can draft objectives, summarize interviews, propose scenarios, produce first-pass visuals, create alternate explanations, generate question banks, restructure a transcript, and scaffold a prototype in minutes. Those capabilities are useful. They are also easy to misuse because the output often arrives in polished language that feels more certain than the underlying evidence deserves.

In learning design, that matters. A polished but weak objective can send an entire build in the wrong direction. A plausible scenario can teach the wrong policy. A confident explanation can contain a subtle factual error. A synthetic learner persona can appear research-based while actually reflecting the model’s assumptions. The danger is not simply hallucination. The deeper danger is authority drift: the moment when a tool that was supposed to assist a decision begins making the decision.

UNESCO’s guidance on generative AI in education is explicitly human-centred and calls for ethical validation, pedagogical design, privacy protection, and human capacity rather than simple technology adoption. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile similarly frame trustworthy AI as a governance problem involving measurement, accountability, monitoring, and risk controls—not merely model performance. Those principles translate directly into learning design practice.

Portfolio lensA mature AI workflow should make it easy for a reviewer to see what the AI proposed, what the designer accepted, what the designer rejected, and what evidence justified the final choice. That audit trail is more impressive than a claim that “AI was used.”

A five-zone model for AI-supported learning design

I use five zones to decide how much authority AI should receive in a workflow. The zones are not based on whether a task is “creative” or “technical.” They are based on the cost of being wrong, the quality of available evidence, and the degree to which professional judgment must remain accountable.

ZoneAI roleHuman responsibilityExamples
1. ExpandGenerate options rapidlyChoose the directionBrainstorming, alternate hooks, possible examples, visual concepts
2. TransformReformat or adapt known materialVerify fidelitySummaries, reading-level variants, transcripts, tables, question stems
3. DraftCreate a first working versionRevise against evidence and standardsObjectives, scenarios, facilitator notes, practice activities
4. AnalyzeSurface patterns or hypothesesValidate against source dataInterview coding, gap themes, feedback clustering, content inventories
5. DecideProvide recommendations onlyRetain decision authorityAssessment validity, policy interpretation, accessibility sign-off, high-stakes content

The model is intentionally conservative in Zone 5. If a decision affects compliance, safety, employee performance ratings, credentialing, student progression, disability access, or sensitive data, AI can assist with analysis but should not be the unreviewed authority.

What an accountable AI workflow actually looks like

Start with the performance requirement.Define what someone must do differently after the intervention. If the desired behavior is vague, faster content generation only accelerates ambiguity.
Ground the model in approved evidence.Use policy, SME input, product documentation, learner data, research, and existing standards. Separate source material from model-generated suggestions.
Ask for alternatives, not one answer.AI is most useful when it widens the solution space. Request multiple scenario structures, examples, explanations, or assessment approaches and compare them.
Apply a human review rubric.Check factual accuracy, alignment, cognitive demand, accessibility, tone, bias, feasibility, and organizational context before anything reaches a learner.
Test with representative users.AI cannot substitute for observing whether real people interpret directions correctly, make the intended decisions, or transfer learning to the job.
Keep the evidence trail.Record source links, decisions, major revisions, and known limitations. This makes later updates faster and protects the integrity of the design.

Prompt quality matters less than design quality

Prompt engineering is useful, but it is often overemphasized. A beautifully structured prompt cannot rescue a weak analysis. If the designer does not know the audience, the performance context, the constraints, the acceptable evidence, or the criterion for success, the model is forced to invent those things. The result can be eloquent and still be wrong.

A stronger pattern is to build prompts from a design brief:

WDW prompt scaffoldContext: What situation are we designing for?
Performance: What must the learner be able to do?
Evidence: What sources may the model rely on?
Constraints: What must it avoid or preserve?
Output: What format is useful for review?
Challenge: What should the model critique in its own answer?

The final line matters. Asking a model to identify assumptions, missing evidence, possible misconceptions, or weak points is often more valuable than asking it to make the original draft longer.

The seven checks before AI-generated learning content ships

  1. Accuracy: Can every important factual claim be traced to a reliable source?
  2. Alignment: Does the practice require the same kind of thinking or performance expected in the real task?
  3. Difficulty: Is the task challenging for the right reason, or merely confusing?
  4. Feedback: Does feedback explain consequences and reasoning rather than just announce right or wrong?
  5. Accessibility: Are alternatives, contrast, reading order, language demands, and interaction requirements appropriate?
  6. Context: Does the language match the organization, role, culture, and actual tools people use?
  7. Evidence of transfer: Is there a plan to observe whether the learning changes performance after completion?

Automation is not the same as autonomy

Workflow automation can save enormous amounts of time without giving an AI system independent decision authority. A transcript can be routed to a folder automatically. A reviewed article can trigger social drafts. Approved narration text can move to a voice tool. Analytics can be collected and summarized. Those are automated handoffs.

Autonomy is different. Autonomy means the system selects goals, makes consequential decisions, or publishes without meaningful review. For many learning-production tasks, the highest-value pattern is not “full agent.” It is a carefully designed sequence where repetitive transfers are automated and judgment gates remain human.

What leaders should ask before buying another AI learning tool

The market encourages organizations to compare feature lists. A more useful evaluation begins with workflow friction. Which step is slow? Which step is repetitive? Which step requires scarce expertise? What data would enter the tool? What happens when the output is wrong? Can the organization export its work? Can a human inspect and revise the result? Is the improvement large enough to justify another platform?

That is why the WDW AI Command Center is organized around workflows rather than an endless directory. A tool earns a place because it helps a specific stage of work. Newness alone is not a criterion.

Frequently asked questions

Should instructional designers disclose AI use?

For portfolio work and professional deliverables, disclosure is useful when AI materially shaped the artifact. The more important disclosure is what the designer remained responsible for: source validation, design decisions, accessibility, revision, and final quality.

Can AI write complete courses?

It can generate large amounts of course-shaped content. That is not the same as diagnosing a performance problem, designing valid practice, testing transfer, or ensuring organizational accuracy. The bottleneck is judgment, not word production.

What should stay human?

Decisions with high consequences, ambiguous evidence, ethical implications, sensitive learner data, or substantial context dependence should retain explicit human ownership.
