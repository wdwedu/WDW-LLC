Feedback That Changes Performance

What effective feedback actually does, why more comments are not necessarily better, and how to design feedback that produces a stronger next attempt.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…
Evidence → next moveFeedback must change action
Key takeawayFeedback is only instructionally powerful when it changes what the learner notices, understands, decides, or does next. The design target is not a better comment. It is a better next attempt.

Feedback is not the comment. Feedback is the change it produces.

Early in my teaching career, I thought I was giving strong feedback because I wrote a lot. I circled things, underlined things, left questions in the margins, added encouragement, and sometimes returned a page that looked as if two people had been writing on it. The problem was obvious once I watched what learners actually did next. Many read the grade, glanced at the comments, and moved on. My effort was visible. Their learning response often was not.

That experience changed the way I think about feedback. The useful question is not, How much feedback did I give? It is, What did the learner do differently because of it? Feedback earns its place when it closes a gap between current performance and a clearer, stronger, more independent performance. Everything else is commentary.

This distinction matters in schools, workplace learning, coaching, eLearning, AI-supported tutoring, and performance management. A beautifully phrased note that arrives too late, overwhelms the learner, points to ten problems at once, or never requires a response may be technically accurate and instructionally weak.

The three questions every useful feedback system must answer

A practical feedback system should help the learner answer three questions: Where am I going? How am I doing? What should I do next? Those questions sound simple, but they impose discipline on the designer. “Where am I going?” requires a visible target. “How am I doing?” requires evidence connected to that target. “What should I do next?” requires an actionable move, not a vague judgment.

The feedback loopTarget → Attempt → Evidence → Next move → New attempt.

If the loop ends at “Evidence,” feedback becomes a report. If it reaches “New attempt,” feedback becomes part of learning.

This is why the word good is rarely useful feedback by itself. So is “needs work.” Both describe the evaluator’s reaction more than they help the learner regulate performance. Compare that with: “Your claim is clear. The evidence is relevant, but you have not explained why it supports the claim. Add one sentence that makes the reasoning explicit, then reread the paragraph without looking at your notes.” Now the learner knows what quality looks like and what to do.

Timing matters because memory and action have windows

Immediate feedback is not always best, and delayed feedback is not always bad. The timing should match the task. If a learner is practicing a dangerous procedure, waiting two days to correct an unsafe step would be irresponsible. If a learner is solving a complex problem, interrupting every productive struggle with a hint can turn feedback into dependency.

Designers should ask what the learner needs at that moment. Does the error need immediate correction because it will compound? Would a short delay force retrieval and self-evaluation? Is the learner still mentally connected to the attempt? Can the feedback arrive before the next performance opportunity? Timing is less about speed than about instructional usefulness.

In digital learning, this means we should stop treating the “Correct/Incorrect” popup as a universal solution. A novice may need direct explanatory feedback. A more experienced learner may benefit from consequence-based feedback, comparison with an expert model, or a second attempt before seeing the answer.

Too much feedback can become another source of cognitive load

One of the easiest mistakes for knowledgeable people to make is correcting everything they notice. Experts see many dimensions at once. Learners usually cannot act on all of them at once. When a paragraph has weak reasoning, inconsistent formatting, grammar problems, missing evidence, and awkward transitions, correcting all five may feel thorough and still produce little improvement.

I prefer a hierarchy: first correct what blocks meaning, then what affects the performance goal, then what can wait. In a scenario, the priority might be judgment. In a writing task, it might be argument structure. In a software simulation, it might be the decision sequence rather than mouse precision.

Design ruleFeedback should be selective enough to act on, specific enough to understand, and demanding enough to require the learner to do some of the thinking.

Specificity without over-directing

There is a difference between useful specificity and doing the task for the learner. “Add a statistic from paragraph three and put it after your second sentence” may fix the product but teach little judgment. “Your claim needs stronger evidence. Choose the source detail that most directly supports the causal relationship you are arguing” preserves more of the thinking.

The right level of specificity depends on expertise. Beginners often need clearer scaffolds because they do not yet know what to attend to. As competence grows, feedback can move from directive to facilitative. The expert coach asks better questions because the learner has enough knowledge to answer them.

This progression is important when we use AI for feedback. A model can generate a polished revision instantly. That is not automatically feedback. Sometimes the stronger instructional move is to have the system identify one weakness, explain the criterion, ask the learner to revise, and only then compare the revision with a model.

Task, process, self-regulation, and the trap of praise

Feedback can operate at different levels. Task feedback addresses whether a response is accurate or complete. Process feedback addresses the strategy used. Self-regulation feedback helps the learner monitor, plan, and evaluate independently. Personal praise is about the person. All four feel different, and they do not have the same learning value.

“You are smart” can feel good and still tell the learner nothing about what produced success. “You checked your first assumption against the data before choosing a recommendation” points to a repeatable process. The second comment is more portable. The learner can use it next time.

Praise is not the enemy. Human beings need encouragement. The point is to avoid using praise as a substitute for information. A supportive learning environment can say, “That revision is much clearer,” and then explain why.

Feedback should make error safe enough to examine

People learn less from mistakes they are busy hiding. This is especially important in professional environments where status, evaluation, and job security can make every error feel expensive. If a simulation punishes the learner theatrically, the learner may focus on avoiding embarrassment rather than examining the decision.

A strong scenario says, in effect, “That choice had consequences. Let us inspect the cues you used, the cues you missed, and what an expert might notice.” It treats error as evidence about thinking. That is a much more productive stance than simply revealing the correct button.

The same principle applies in classrooms. When students become experts at guessing what the teacher wants, we may get compliance without stronger reasoning. Feedback should help them see quality, not merely please the evaluator.

Peer feedback works only when quality is visible

Peer review is often assigned as if learners automatically know how to review. They do not. If the instruction is “Give your partner feedback,” we should not be surprised when the result is “Looks good.” Peer feedback becomes more useful when learners have a clear criterion, examples, sentence stems, or a short diagnostic task.

One structure I like is Notice, Name, Next. Notice one observable feature. Name the criterion it affects. Suggest or ask for one next move. “I notice the example is detailed. The criterion asks us to connect evidence to the claim. What sentence could make that connection explicit?” That is manageable and teaches the reviewer at the same time.

In the workplace, feedback must connect to the performance environment

Workplace feedback fails when it ignores the system around the performer. Telling an employee to “be more efficient” does not help if the software requires duplicate entry, approvals take three days, or the employee has never been told which tasks are priorities. Feedback about performance should separate skill from environment.

This is where instructional design and performance consulting meet. If the person knows what to do and wants to do it but the workflow blocks them, training and feedback are not the primary solution. If the person cannot recognize the cues that distinguish one situation from another, practice and feedback may be exactly what is needed.

Slow completion“Work faster.”Check process friction, tool delays, priorities, then skill.
Repeated judgment error“Be more careful.”Identify missed cues and rehearse discrimination.
Inconsistent quality“Do better.”Make the quality standard visible and compare examples.
Low transfer“Remember the training.”Practice across varied contexts and provide job support.

AI makes feedback abundant. That does not make it good.

Generative AI can produce feedback in seconds, which changes the economics of response. It can comment on drafts, simulate customers, ask follow-up questions, produce alternative examples, and provide practice on demand. That is powerful. It also creates a new problem: learners can receive more feedback than they can evaluate.

AI feedback should therefore be constrained by criteria. The system should know the goal, the learner’s level, what evidence matters, what it should not infer, and when a human should review the response. In consequential situations, a fluent model should not become the final evaluator simply because it is available.

The human designer still decides what quality means. The model can help scale the conversation.

A practical feedback design protocol

  1. Define the performance. What should the learner be able to do without support?
  2. Make quality visible. Use criteria, examples, models, or observable indicators.
  3. Capture an attempt. Feedback without learner performance becomes advice.
  4. Diagnose the gap. Is the problem knowledge, strategy, attention, environment, or misunderstanding?
  5. Choose one next move. Prioritize the feedback the learner can act on now.
  6. Require response. Revision, explanation, retry, comparison, or new application.
  7. Fade support. Move responsibility toward the learner as competence grows.

The moment I stopped grading and started designing the next attempt

The biggest shift in my own practice was mental. I stopped seeing feedback as the end of an assignment and started seeing it as the design of the next attempt. That changed what I wrote, when I wrote it, and how much I wrote. It also changed the assignments themselves. If there was no meaningful opportunity to use the feedback, I had to ask whether the timing of the task made sense.

That same shift applies to corporate learning and eLearning. A quiz result is not the end. A scenario consequence is not the end. A coaching note is not the end. The important question is what the learner does next while the evidence is still usable.

The best feedback increases independence

Good feedback does not make the learner permanently dependent on a teacher, coach, manager, or AI assistant. It helps the learner internalize standards, notice patterns, monitor performance, and eventually generate better feedback for themselves.

That is the finish line. Not more comments. Better judgment.
