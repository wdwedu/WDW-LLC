The Performance Gap: Knowledge, Environment, Motivation, or Process?

A practical diagnostic for separating skill problems from system problems before a learning team prescribes another course.

By Wayne WilliamsLast reviewed: September 2026Calculating read time…
Diagnose before designKnowledge · environment · motivation · process
Key takeawayA request for training is not proof of a learning problem. Diagnose the gap across knowledge, environment, motivation, expectations, tools, process, and consequences before deciding what to build.

A performance gap is not a training request

“They need training” is one of the most common sentences in learning and development, and one of the most dangerous. It sounds like a diagnosis, but it is usually a proposed solution. Before a designer opens PowerPoint, Storyline, a video editor, or an AI tool, we need to know what is actually happening.

A performance gap is the difference between what people are doing now and what successful performance requires. That gap can be caused by missing knowledge or skill. It can also be caused by unclear expectations, poor tools, broken processes, competing incentives, missing resources, weak feedback, workload, or a system that makes the desired behavior harder than the undesired one.

In schools I learned this the practical way. When students did not perform, it was tempting to reteach immediately. Sometimes that was exactly right. Other times the issue was not knowledge. The directions were unclear. The reading load buried the concept. Students could explain the idea orally but not in the required format. Or the assignment measured something different from what I thought I had taught. The same diagnostic humility belongs in workplace learning.

Four causes I check before I recommend training

I group performance barriers into four working categories: knowledge and skill, environment and process, motivation and consequence, and role clarity or expectations. Real problems can cross categories, but the structure prevents us from treating every gap as a memory problem.

CauseWhat it sounds likeLikely response
Knowledge or skill“I do not know how.”Instruction, practice, coaching, job aids
Environment or process“The system makes it difficult.”Workflow, tools, resources, process redesign
Motivation or consequence“There is no reason to do it that way.”Incentives, consequences, culture, leadership
Clarity or expectation“I did not know that was the standard.”Communication, examples, standards, manager alignment

Start with evidence, not interviews alone

Stakeholder interviews matter, but stakeholders usually experience the problem through their role. A manager may see carelessness. An employee may see an impossible process. A customer may see inconsistency. The designer needs multiple forms of evidence.

I want performance data, examples of strong and weak work, observations of the task, the tools people use, the instructions they receive, the time available, common errors, and what happens after good or poor performance. If the organization has no data, that itself becomes a design finding.

ATD’s performance-consulting guidance emphasizes systematic analysis and root-cause work before recommending solutions. The habit is simple: describe the current state, define the desired state, then test explanations for the difference.

Define desired performance as something observable

“Improve customer service” is not a performance definition. “Resolve routine account questions in one interaction while accurately documenting the reason, action, and follow-up” is closer. Once performance is observable, we can ask what knowledge, cues, decisions, tools, and conditions make it possible.

The desired state should be specific enough that two reasonable observers can recognize it. If nobody can describe strong performance, the training team cannot build toward it.

Study the people who are already succeeding

One of the fastest ways to understand a gap is to compare typical performers with people who consistently succeed under the same conditions. What do strong performers notice? What sequence do they follow? What shortcuts have they learned? Which resources do they use? How do they recover when something goes wrong?

This comparison often reveals tacit knowledge. Experts may not describe their thinking unless we ask about specific cases. “What did you notice first?” and “What made you choose that option?” are often better questions than “What is your process?”

Performance is produced by a system

Instructional designers naturally focus on the learner. Performance consultants widen the frame. People work inside systems of tools, policies, incentives, deadlines, information, managers, customers, and other teams. The same person can perform well in one system and poorly in another.

If the CRM requires ten screens to complete a simple action, a faster course will not fix the workflow. If managers reward speed but the training emphasizes careful documentation, the consequence system is fighting the learning system. If the job aid is buried in a portal no one can search, memory becomes an unnecessary bottleneck.

Performance equationCapability × Opportunity × Clarity × Support × Consequence

If one factor approaches zero, training alone may have little effect.

Do not diagnose motivation until you inspect the environment

“They just do not care” is an attractive explanation because it ends the analysis. Motivation is real, but behavior usually makes more sense when we examine consequences. What happens when someone performs correctly? What happens when they take a shortcut? Which behavior is easier, faster, safer, praised, or punished?

If the organization says quality matters but rewards only volume, employees receive the real curriculum through the incentive system. People learn what the environment reinforces.

When training is the right answer

Training earns its place when people need knowledge, discrimination, procedure, judgment, or skill that they cannot reliably perform now and when practice plus feedback can reasonably close the gap.

That may mean learning a new regulation, rehearsing a difficult conversation, recognizing risk indicators, using a new system, troubleshooting exceptions, or applying a decision framework. The learning design should then mirror the actual performance rather than merely present information about it.

Sometimes the right answer is a job aid

If information changes frequently, is used rarely, or does not need to be memorized, a performance-support tool may outperform a course. Checklists, decision trees, templates, search tools, examples, calculators, and embedded prompts can put information where the work happens.

The question is not whether people can memorize the material. The question is whether memorization is required for successful performance. If not, forcing memory may add cost without adding value.

Most real solutions are mixed

A mature performance solution often combines learning and non-learning interventions. A new process may require training, a simplified job aid, manager coaching, revised metrics, and changes to the software. The learning team should not be embarrassed by that. It means the diagnosis was bigger than a course.

The goal is not to maximize training. The goal is to improve performance.

A simple case: the employee who keeps making the same error

Imagine a team repeatedly entering the wrong code for a small but important class of customer cases. The first response is to assign refresher training. Before doing that, we observe the workflow.

We discover that the codes have nearly identical names, the correct distinction depends on one detail buried in a long policy page, and the software presents no reminder at the point of entry. Strong performers have created their own sticky-note comparison chart.

Now the solution changes. We may still teach the distinction, but we also redesign the job aid, place the comparison at the moment of decision, and rename or clarify the options if the system allows it. Training becomes one layer in a better performance environment.

A practical performance-gap interview

  1. What result should be happening?
  2. What is happening now?
  3. How do we know there is a gap?
  4. Who performs well, and under what conditions?
  5. What knowledge or skill is required?
  6. What tools, information, time, and resources are required?
  7. What makes the desired behavior easier or harder?
  8. What consequences follow good and poor performance?
  9. What part of the gap can training realistically change?
  10. How will we know performance improved?

AI can speed analysis, but it cannot observe your organization for you

AI can summarize interviews, cluster error patterns, draft questions, analyze documents, and help a consultant generate hypotheses. It cannot magically know which hypothesis is true. If the data is incomplete or filtered through stakeholder assumptions, the output will be polished uncertainty.

Use AI to expand the analysis, not replace direct evidence. Observation, performance data, work samples, and conversations with performers remain essential.

Training becomes more valuable when we stop prescribing it automatically

Performance consulting does not reduce the importance of instructional design. It protects it. When we reserve training for problems learning can actually solve, courses become more relevant, evaluation becomes clearer, and stakeholders begin to see the learning team as a performance partner rather than a content factory.

The strongest designer is not the person who can build a course for every request. It is the person who knows when a course is the right tool.

Observation is often the fastest way to destroy a bad assumption

People describe work differently from how work actually happens. Policies describe the official process. Experts skip steps they no longer notice. Managers see outcomes but not always the obstacles. Observation reveals the gap between the designed process and the lived one.

Watch a few performers complete the task. Ask them to think aloud at key decisions. Notice where they pause, switch tools, ask a colleague, create a workaround, or ignore the official documentation. Those moments are performance data.

Quantify the gap when possible

“Performance is poor” is hard to design against. How poor? Compared with what? What is the error rate, cycle time, rework, customer impact, quality score, or missed opportunity? Even approximate numbers help prioritize.

Not every gap deserves a learning project. A small low-consequence gap may cost more to fix than to tolerate. Performance consulting includes deciding which problems are worth solving.

Do not average away the pattern

If overall performance is 80 percent, the average may hide important groups. New employees may be at 55 percent while experienced employees are at 95. One location may be struggling because of a local process. One task subtype may produce most errors.

Segment by role, tenure, context, tool, task type, and other relevant conditions. The intervention changes when the gap is concentrated rather than universal.

Look for positive deviance

Sometimes a few people succeed despite the same difficult conditions. Study them. They may have invented a workaround, mental shortcut, phrase, checklist, or sequence that the formal system never captured.

Performance improvement can begin by making hidden good practice visible.

Motivation deserves evidence too

If people know how and have the resources but still do not perform, examine consequences. Is the desired behavior slower? Does it create more work? Are managers modeling something different? Is there social risk? Is the reward attached to another metric?

Motivation is often rational behavior inside the current system.

Ask whether the process would allow an excellent person to succeed

Imagine your best performer enters the workflow today. Do they have the time, tools, authority, and information needed? If even an expert would struggle, the process is probably part of the problem.

This question helps prevent organizations from trying to train people around structural failure.

A performance gap is a hypothesis until evidence explains it

The visible difference between current and desired performance tells us where to look. It does not tell us why the difference exists. The why must be earned through evidence.

That discipline is what separates needs analysis from content production.

The diagnostic conversation I want before anybody asks for a course

One of the fastest ways to improve a learning project is to change the first conversation. Instead of beginning with, “What content needs to be in the course?” begin with, “What is happening now that should be happening differently?” That question sounds simple, but it changes the entire posture of the work. It moves the designer away from being an order taker and toward being a performance partner.

In practice, I want the requester to describe a real moment. What does the employee, student, customer, or team member need to do? What do strong performers do in that same moment? What usually goes wrong? What happens immediately before the mistake? What happens afterward? What does the person see on the screen, hear from a supervisor, or have available at the point of need? Those questions create a picture of performance that content lists cannot.

The harder question is whether they can recognize the right information, retrieve it when needed, and act under the real conditions of the task.

A five-question performance interview

QuestionWhat it reveals
What should a strong performer do?The observable target instead of a vague learning goal.
What happens now?The size and shape of the actual gap.
What gets in the way?Environmental, process, tool, incentive, or knowledge barriers.
Who already succeeds?Positive deviance and hidden practices worth studying.
What evidence would prove improvement?The evaluation measure before the intervention is built.

Triangulate before you prescribe

No single source gives the whole answer. Managers may describe a motivation problem. Employees may describe an impossible process. The data may show the errors are concentrated among new hires. Observation may reveal that everyone is using a workaround because the official procedure is too slow. Each source is useful, but each is incomplete.

A practical diagnosis combines at least three perspectives when the stakes justify it: what people say, what people do, and what the performance evidence shows. Interviews and surveys reveal perceptions. Observation reveals behavior. Operational measures reveal patterns over time. When those three agree, confidence rises. When they disagree, the disagreement itself becomes the most interesting finding.

Build an intervention matrix, not a training wish list

Once the causes are clearer, match the response to the cause. A knowledge problem may need instruction. A memory problem may need retrieval practice or a job aid. A tool problem may need interface redesign. A process problem may need fewer steps. A motivation problem may need different incentives, feedback, or leadership behavior. Many real performance gaps need a combination.

Primary causeFirst response to considerPossible learning support
Knowledge or skillPractice and feedbackScenario, demonstration, coached rehearsal
Memory at point of needPerformance supportChecklist, decision aid, searchable reference
Broken processProcess redesignBrief orientation to the new workflow
Tool frictionTool or interface improvementEmbedded guidance only where needed
Incentive conflictAlign consequencesManager communication and reinforcement
Unclear expectationsClarify standardsExamples, criteria, calibration practice

The discipline here is not anti-training. It is pro-effectiveness. Training becomes more valuable when it is reserved for the parts of the problem that learning can actually change. That produces shorter courses, better practice, clearer evaluation, and more credibility with the people who asked for help.

Why this matters in an instructional-design portfolio

A portfolio filled only with polished screens can prove that someone can build. It does not automatically prove that they can diagnose. Showing the reasoning behind an intervention is often the stronger professional signal. What was the performance requirement? What evidence suggested the cause? Why was a scenario chosen instead of a reference guide? Why was part of the request solved without training at all?

That thinking is what I want visible throughout wdw.llc. The artifact matters, but the decision that produced the artifact matters more. A learning designer becomes more useful when the organization can trust that the solution was selected because it fits the problem, not because it happens to be the tool the designer already knows how to build.

A simple case shows why diagnosis changes the solution

Imagine a customer-service team with a rising number of incorrect account changes. The initial request is a refresher course on the procedure. Interviews reveal that employees can explain the correct procedure. Observation shows that the system times out during a critical verification step, so experienced employees have developed a workaround. Newer employees follow the official process and are more likely to lose progress. A course would add information to people who already know the rule while leaving the failure condition intact.

The better response might combine a technical fix, a temporary job aid for the workaround, and a short communication explaining the revised process. The learning component becomes smaller because the diagnosis became better.

Now change one variable

Suppose the system works perfectly, but employees skip verification because they are measured primarily on call speed. The same visible error rate now has a different cause. Training may again be part of the response, but the performance environment must change too. If the metric rewards speed over accuracy, the organization is teaching one behavior while the course teaches another.

This is why performance gaps are not content gaps until evidence says they are. The visible symptom may be identical while the correct intervention is completely different.

The designer’s role is to make the invisible cause visible

Good analysis often feels less glamorous than development because there is no polished screen to show yet. But it is the stage where the project earns its direction. The designer helps stakeholders move from a complaint to an observable performance statement, from a performance statement to evidence, from evidence to plausible causes, and from causes to interventions that actually fit.

That reasoning should be visible in a portfolio because it is one of the strongest signals that a designer can work beyond software. Tools build the artifact. Diagnosis determines whether the artifact should exist.

End with a testable hypothesis

Before choosing the intervention, write one sentence that explains the gap in a way evidence could prove wrong. For example: “New employees make more account-change errors because the verification step is interrupted by system timeout, not because they do not know the procedure.” A testable hypothesis forces the team to separate what it knows from what it assumes.

If later evidence contradicts the hypothesis, change the solution. That flexibility is not failure. It is what disciplined diagnosis looks like. The purpose of analysis is not to defend the first explanation. It is to get close enough to the real cause that the intervention has a fair chance to work.
